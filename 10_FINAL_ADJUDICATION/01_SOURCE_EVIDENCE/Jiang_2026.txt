A B S T R A C T
Background: and Purpose: Postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD) is a significant complication 
following abdominal surgery and is characterized by delayed flatus and stool passage, postponed oral feeding, 
and dyspeptic symptoms. The study aimed to evaluate whether transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation 
(TEAS) accelerates gastrointestinal function recovery and reduces POGD-related complications after elective 
laparoscopic non-gastrointestinal surgery.
Materials and methods: Patients scheduled for selective non-gastrointestinal laparoscopic surgery were random­
ized into the TEAS or sham-TEAS group. TEAS group patients were administered electrical stimulation at acu­
points Zusanli (ST36), Neiguan (PC6), and Hegu (LI4) starting 30 min prior to anesthesia induction and 
continuing until surgery completion. Patients in the sham-TEAS group received a sham stimulus at the same 
acupoints, and no output current was delivered by disconnecting the device's output line. Our primary endpoint 
included the time to first flatus, whereas the secondary endpoints were the time to first defecation, postoperative 
nausea and vomiting (PONV) rate, length of hospital stay, hospital expenses, numeric rating scale (NRS) pain 
score, and incidence of postoperative complications.
Results: A total of 587 patients were enrolled and assigned to the TEAS (n = 294) or sham-TEAS (n = 293) group 
between May 15, 2019, and October 18, 2021. Compared with the sham-TEAS group, the TEAS group had a 
significantly shorter time to first flatus (median [interquartile range (IQR)], 21.50 h [19.0–23.5 h] vs. 23.5 h 
[21.0–26.0 h]; p < 0.001) and time to first defecation (41.2 h [38.5–45.0 h] vs. 46.0 h [42.0–52.0 h]; p < 0.001). 
Additionally, the TEAS group showed lower rates of PONV (15% vs. 22.9%; p = 0.015) and abdominal distention 
(21.1% vs. 29.7%; p = 0.017), as well as lower NRS pain scores (median [IQR], 3 [2–3] vs. 3 [2–3.5]; p = 0.026). 
There were no significant differences in hospital stay length and hospitalization expenses.
Conclusion: TEAS accelerates gastrointestinal function recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic non- 
gastrointestinal surgery, reducing PONV, abdominal distention and postoperative pain without increasing hos­
pital stay or costs.
1. Introduction
Compared with open surgery, laparoscopic surgery offers advantages 
such as less surgical pain, shorter hospital stays, fewer infections, and 
faster return to normal activities [1–3]. However, the creation of 
pneumoperitoneum, the application of anaesthetics, and mechanical 
punctures during laparoscopic procedures can disrupt gastrointestinal 
peristalsis. This disruption can subsequently lead to postoperative 
complications, including nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, anal 
sphincter dysfunction, defecation difficulties, and delayed physical re­
covery [4,5]. Postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD) signif­
icantly impairs patient recovery, prolongs hospital stays, and increases

the risk of additional complications, with an estimated annual economic 
burden of approximately $1.5 billion in the USA [6]. Postoperative ileus 
(POI), as the most common clinical manifestation of POGD, remains a 
critical concern for abdominal surgeons due to its high incidence and 
clinical impact [7].
The pathophysiology of POGD is complex and multifactorial, 
involving neurogenic, inflammatory, and pharmacological mechanisms, 
including sympathetic overactivation, cytokine release, and opioid use, 
all of which delay gastrointestinal motility [6]. It is generally believed 
that the early phase of the reaction to surgery is mediated neurally and 
involves neural reflexes activated during and immediately after surgery. 
An incision in the skin elevates adrenergic motor neuronal activity, 
leading to acute intestinal paralysis. After 3 to 4 h of surgical manipu­
lation, the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines 
up-regulates endothelial adhesion molecules. This cascade activates 
phagocytes residing throughout the gut, leading to leukocyte migration 
into the muscularis externa. Ultimately, these phagocytes release nitric 
oxide and prostaglandins, which directly inhibit smooth muscle 
contractility to prevent intestinal peristalsis [8]. In addition, the use of 
opioids, fluid overload, and gastrointestinal tract edema can all 
adversely affect gastrointestinal function, thereby prolonging recovery 
[7].
Therefore, current management strategies employ a multimodal 
approach that integrates pharmacological treatments with non­
pharmacological interventions [8,9]. However, the high cost and po­
tential adverse effects of pharmacological treatments can reduce their 
acceptability [10]. Alternative strategies focus on enhancing post­
operative rehabilitation through interventions such as patient educa­
tion, chewing gum, limited preoperative fasting, minimally invasive 
surgical techniques, early oral feeding, and effective analgesia [11–13]. 
Despite these approaches, no clinical guidelines or recommendations 
currently specify drugs or interventions tailored to preventing POGD.
In line with traditional Chinese medicine principles, surgical in­
terventions are believed to induce Qi stagnation and blood stasis syn­
drome, disrupting the body's balance and leading to bowel dysfunction 
[14]. Acupuncture, through stimulation of specific acupoints, is pro­
posed to regulate energy (Qi) flow and alleviate blood stasis, making it 
widely utilized for managing gastrointestinal disorders because of its 
efficacy and minimal invasion [14–16]. However, the reliance on skilled 
practitioners for traditional acupuncture and moxibustion limits its 
widespread application. Transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation 
(TEAS) offers a non-invasive alternative to conventional acupuncture, 
employing electrical stimulation through surface electrodes instead of 
needle insertion and manual manipulation, as in electroacupuncture. 
TEAS has demonstrated clinical benefits, including reduced intra­
operative anesthetic requirements, decreased postoperative nausea and 
vomiting [5], alleviated postoperative pain [17], and improved post­
operative recovery [18]. Moreover, previous research has shown that 
TEAS enhances gastrointestinal recovery by activating the vagal nerve 
and inhibiting inflammation, as evidenced by increased plasma levels of 
acetylcholine (Ach) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) alongside decreased 
levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) 
[19].
Although several rigorously designed randomized controlled trials 
(RCTs) [4,5,14,17,19,20] have evaluated TEAS for postoperative 
gastrointestinal dysfunction, there remains significant variability in the 
definitions of POI and POGD, as well as in clinical outcome measures for 
assessing gastrointestinal recovery. Furthermore, existing guidelines for 
the prevention and management of POGD have not yet incorporated 
recommendations for TEAS or electroacupuncture. Therefore, this study 
was conducted to evaluate whether TEAS, as an adjunctive 
non-pharmacological intervention, could effectively promote gastroin­
testinal function recovery and reduce the incidence of postoperative 
nausea and vomiting (PONV) and abdominal distension in patients un­
dergoing elective laparoscopic non-gastrointestinal surgery.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Study design
The present prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled clin­
ical trial was carried out at the tertiary Nankai Hospital from May 15, 
2019, to October 18, 2021. The study was carried out with a TEAS group 
and a sham-TEAS group.
2.2. Patients
Eligible patients were those aged 18–64 years, with American Soci­
ety of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I–III disease, and planned to undergo 
selective laparoscopic non-gastrointestinal surgery. Patients with a 
previous history of abdominal surgery, gastrointestinal motility disor­
ders, severe cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal disease, presence of a 
cardiac pacemaker, infection at acupuncture sites, allergy to acupunc­
ture electrode patches, prior acupuncture treatment, or participation in 
other studies within three months preceding this trial were excluded.
2.3. Randomization and blinding
The principal investigator explained more details of this study to the 
enrolled patients one day prior to their scheduled surgery, in accordance 
with a standardized perioperative protocol. Laparoscopic non- 
gastrointestinal surgeries were conducted under general anesthesia. 
Patients who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled in this study and 
followed up postoperatively.
Patients were randomized into the TEAS or sham-TEAS groups at a 
1:1 ratio using block randomization. The randomization sequence was 
generated by an independent statistician using computer software. The 
acupuncturist, the sole individual aware of treatment allocations, 
accessed the randomization sequence through sealed opaque envelopes 
containing computer-generated allocations. Patients, outcome assessors, 
and statisticians were blinded to group assignments.
2.4. Interventions
Acupoints were selected through a comprehensively review of the 
literature and expert consensus. Precise acupoint locations were deter­
mined using The National Standards for Acupoint Location (GB/T 
12346–2021 nomenclature and location of meridian points) guidelines 
[21]. A licensed acupuncturist with nine years of clinical experience, 
registered as a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) practitioner, inde­
pendently administered either TEAS or sham-TEAS. The acupuncturist 
did not disclose group allocation but informed patients that tingling 
sensations might have occurred near the wrists and calves during 
treatment. In the TEAS group, acupoints associated with managing 
abdominal distension, abdominal pain, and constipation, such as Zusanli 
(stomach meridian ST36), Neiguan (pericardium meridian PC6), and 
Hegu (large intestine meridian LI4), were identified. The bilateral acu­
point skin was cleaned with alcohol and dried, and electrodes were 
attached to the TEAS machine (Hua Tuo SDZ-III, Suzhou Medical 
Apparatus and Instruments Factory, Suzhou, China). The TEAS group 
was administered at a 2/10 Hz dense-disperse frequency, starting 
30 min before anesthesia induction and continuing through the opera­
tion. We adjusted the stimulation intensity to induce mild muscle 
twitching at the individual's maximum tolerance, achieving the radi­
ating paresthesia sensation known as de qi [22]. In the sham-TEAS 
group, identical acupoints were targeted, but stimulation was simu­
lated by cutting output wires, ensuring no electrical current delivery 
[23] (eFigure in Supplement 1).
Standardized anesthesia protocols were employed across all patients. 
Patients received 5 mg of dexamethasone prior to induction, followed by 
0.03 mg/kg midazolam, 1.5–2.5 mg/kg propofol, 0.3–0.4 μg/kg sufen­
tanil, and 0.8 mg/kg rocuronium. Maintenance anesthesia consisted of

0.05–0.2 μg/kg/min remifentanil and 4–12 mg/kg/h propofol, with 
intermittent rocuronium administration (0.2 mg/kg) every 30 min. 
Sedation depth was monitored using the bispectral index (BIS), which is 
a quantitative, dimensionless measure derived from processed electro­
encephalogram (EEG) signals and ranges from 0 to 100. BIS is routinely 
used to monitor the depth of anesthesia and sedation in real time, with 
values between 40 and 60 representing a state of general anesthesia 
[24]. In our study, the propofol infusion rate was adjusted to maintain 
BIS values between 40 and 60. The ventilation parameters were adjusted 
to maintain the pressure of end tidal carbon dioxide (PetCO2) level at 
30–40 mmHg. After peritoneal suturing, sufentanil (5–10 μg) was 
administered as needed. Upon skin closure, patients were transferred to 
the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), where extubation was performed 
after spontaneous breathing resumed, with muscle relaxant antagonists 
(1–2 mg neostigmine and 0.5–1 mg atropine) administered as necessary. 
PONV were prevented by administering ondansetron, a 5-hydroxytryp­
tamine-3 (5-HT3) receptor antagonist. Postoperative analgesia included 
sufentanil and flurbiprofen via patient-controlled intravenous analgesia 
(PCIA) for 24–48 h if needed. Adverse events that occurred throughout 
this study were reported.
2.5. Outcome measures
Our primary endpoint included the time to first flatus, which referred 
to the duration between the completion of laparoscopic surgery and its 
occurrence (unit: hour); this was assessed every 6 h postoperatively until 
occurrence or discharge. The secondary endpoints were the time to first 
defecation (assessed at the same time points as the primary endpoint), 
pain score evaluated by the numeric rating scale (NRS; 0 = no pain, 
10 = worst imaginable pain) at 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h postoperatively, and 
PONV incidence at the same three time points. Hospital expenses were 
documented in Chinese yuan (CNY) and converted into US dollars using 
an average exchange rate of 0.1525 from 2019 to 2021. Adverse events, 
whether related to the treatment or not, were reported through patient 
interviews conducted by a research assistant at each follow-up visit.
2.6. Statistical analysis
We determined our sample size in line with one preliminary work 
conducted in 2018 (n = 50), which showed times to first flatus of 
21.5 ± 8.6 h and 23.5 ± 6.7 h for the TEAS and sham groups, respec­
tively. Using a one-sided significance level of α = 0.025 (corresponding 
to a two-sided α = 0.05 for the final analysis) and 80% power, the 
minimum required sample size was determined to be 235 patients per 
group. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of about 20%, a total 
of 294 patients per group were planned. Sample size calculations were 
performed using PASS software, version 15.0 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, 
USA).
Data analysis followed the per-protocol set principle, For the primary 
outcome, an intention-to-treat analysis was also performed. For missing 
data in the TEAS group, values were replaced with the maximum 
observed value (worst-case scenario) from the entire cohort. Conversely, 
missing data in the sham-TEAS group were replaced with the minimum 
observed value (best-case scenario) from the entire cohort. The 
normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov- 
Smirnov test. Continuous variables conforming to the normal distribu­
tion were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) and were 
analyzed using two-tailed Student's t-tests. Continuous variables con­
forming to a non-normal distribution were presented as medians with 
interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared using Mann-Whitney U 
tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and pro­
portions and analyzed using χ2 tests or Fisher's exact tests. The cumu­
lative no-flatus rate was assessed by log-rank tests and Kaplan-Meier 
curves. Comparison of the outcomes between the two groups was pre­
sented with effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p- 
values. A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically 
significant. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 25.0 (IBM 
Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).
2.7. Ethical considerations
Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional 
Review Board of Tianjin Nankai Hospital (NKYY-YX-IRB-2017-035-02), 
and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of 
Helsinki. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration 
number NCT03724656). All patients provided written informed consent 
prior to enrollment and were informed of the possibility of withdrawing 
from the study at any time. Data were anonymized to protect privacy.
3. Results
3.1. Characteristics of patients
There were 1226 patients screened between May 2019 and October 
2021 for eligibility. Of these, 612 were eliminated because of failure to 
meet the inclusion criteria, refusal to take part in this study, or other 
reasons. Ultimately, 614 eligible patients were allocated to the TEAS 
(n = 308) or sham-TEAS (n = 306) group. During this study, 12 and 9 
patients in the TEAS and sham-TEAS groups, respectively, withdrew. 
Thus, 587 patients (TEAS: n = 294; sham-TEAS: n = 293) were included 
in this study and were thereby enrolled in the final analyses. The patient 
selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Patient demographic characteristics, 
comorbidities, surgical data and perioperative anesthetic use were 
compared between the two groups. The results showed that the two 
groups were well balanced on baseline and intra-operative data 
(Tables 1 and 2).
3.2. Primary endpoint
Patients in TEAS group demonstrated a remarkably shorter median 
time to first flatus compared to sham-TEAS group (21.5 h [19.0–23.5] 
vs. 23.5 h [21.0–26.0]; median difference: −2.5 h (95% CI, −3.0 to 
−1.8); p < 0.001). The intention-to-treat analysis found similar results: 
the time to first flatus in TEAS group compared to sham-TEAS group 
(21.3 h [19.0–23.5] vs. 23.4 h [21.0–26.0]; median difference: −2.0 h 
(95% CI, −2.9 to −1.5); p < 0.001) (Table 3). The cumulative no-flatus 
rate was assessed by log-rank tests and Kaplan-Meier curves. Kaplan- 
Meier analysis demonstrated a significantly higher cumulative no- 
flatus rate in the sham-TEAS group compared to the TEAS group (log- 
rank test, P < 0.001). The hazard ratio (HR) for the first flatus was 0.55 
(95% CI, 0.45–0.67), indicating that patients receiving TEAS had a 45% 
higher probability of achieving first flatus at any given time point than 
those receiving sham stimulation (Fig. 2).
3.3. Secondary endpoints and adverse events
Compared with the sham-TEAS group, the TEAS group presented a 
significantly shorter median time to first defecation (41.2 h [38.5–45.0] 
vs. 46.0 h [42.0–52.0], p < 0.001). Additionally, the TEAS group 
demonstrated reduced 24 h PONV incidence (15.0% vs. 22.9%, 
p = 0.015), lower rates of abdominal distension (21.1% vs. 29.7%, 
p = 0.017), and decreased pain intensity (NRS pain score, 3 [2,3] vs. 3 
[2–3.5], p = 0.026). The duration of postoperative hospital stay and 
hospitalization expenses were similar in both groups. Adverse events 
reported during the study were mild and resolved quickly without 
long-term effects (Tables 4 and 5).

with the SA group. In another multicenter randomized clinical trial of 
colorectal surgery, Gao et al. [19] reported that postoperative TEAS 
significantly reduced the rate of postoperative paralytic ileus after

the first in the PACU upon reaching a Steward recovery score of 4, and 
the remaining three once daily between 9 and 11 a.m. on postoperative

gastrointestinal dysfunction remain unstandardized across studies 
investigating TEAS for postoperative gastrointestinal recovery.
POI is characterized by impaired gastrointestinal transit following 
abdominal surgery [7]. Despite significant efforts, a universally 
accepted definition of ileus has not been established. Variability in 
outcome reporting has been identified as a major source of heteroge­
neity in clinical studies. A systematic review [26] highlighted the clin­
ical significance of time to first flatus as a reliable indicator of 
postoperative gastrointestinal recovery, noting its practicality compared 
with the assessment of bowel sounds, which clinicians often find chal­
lenging because of time constraints. Consequently, the time to first flatus 
and defecation remain key metrics for assessing postoperative gastro­
intestinal function and are commonly employed as endpoints in clinical 
trials [13,14,25,27]. We selected time to first flatus as the primary 
endpoint, which is consistent with established practice.
POGD is associated with a complex pathophysiological mechanism 
involving inflammation, fluid administration, medication effects, and 
ischemia-reperfusion injury [26,28,29]. TEAS is widely applied in 
clinical settings as an economical, non-invasive, and straightforward 
approach for managing gastrointestinal disorders, pain, stress urinary 
incontinence, and conditions related to reproductive medicine [17,20,
30–33]. However, the underlying mechanisms of TEAS remain largely 
unclear. Factors such as the number of sessions, selection of acupoints, 
optimal timing, and environmental conditions influence the efficacy of 
electroacupuncture. In this study, the selected acupoints included 
Zusanli (ST36), Neiguan (PC6), and Hegu (LI4). The Zusanli (ST36) 
acupoint, located on the Foot Yang Ming stomach meridian, has an 
important effect on modulating the stomach and spleen while enhancing 
gastrointestinal motility. In modern mechanistic studies, TEAS at ST36 
enhances gastrointestinal recovery by activating the vagus nerve and 
inhibiting inflammation, as evidenced by increased plasma levels of 
acetylcholine and IL-10 alongside decreased levels of IL-6 and iNOS 
[19]. Electroacupuncture at ST36 has been shown to relieve chronic 
constipation by increasing vagal activity and decreasing sympathetic 
activity [34]. Additionally, Liu et al. have reported that electro­
acupuncture at ST36 could relieve gastroesophageal reflux disease by 
modulating autonomic function [35]. The Neiguan (PC6) acupoint, a 
Luo-connecting point on the Pericardium Meridian of Hand Jueyin, can 
effectively alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea and 
vomiting, likely through modulation of the vagus nerve [36]. Similarly, 
Hegu (LI4), a Yuan-source point on the Large Intestinal Meridian of 
Hand-Yangming [37], activates the vagal anti-inflammatory pathway,

This study had several strengths, including large sample size and the 
use of a sham stimulus, which minimized subjective bias in the control 
group and ensured credible findings. However, there are some limita­
tions. First, caution should be exercised when generalizing our findings 
to other populations or settings because of its single-center nature. 
Second, despite strict blinding protocols, the study did not evaluate 
whether the blinding was effective by asking patients to guess their 
assigned stimulation. Third, the use of fixed block randomization 
introduced potential bias due to treatment allocation predictability. 
Fourth, the precise impact of TEAS on gastrointestinal activity remains 
unclear, necessitating further studies to elucidate its mechanisms.
5. Conclusion
In young and middle-aged patients undergoing laparoscopic non- 
gastrointestinal surgery, TEAS significantly reduces time to first flatus 
and defecation, as well as the incidence of PONV and abdominal 
distension, without affecting hospital stay duration or costs. These 
findings suggest that TEAS may be a useful adjunctive therapy in this 
setting. Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms under­
lying such interventions and to validate these results across diverse 
populations and clinical settings.
