ABSTRACT
Objectives To investigate the anaesthetic and
analgesic effect of multipoint transcutaneous
electrical acupuncture stimulation (TEAS) during
supratentorial tumour resection for postoperative
recovery and side effects.
Methods In a blinded clinical trial, 92 patients
scheduled for supratentorial craniotomy under
general anaesthesia were randomly allocated into
either a multipoint TEAS (n=46) or a sham TEAS
group (n=46). All patients received total
intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) with propofol and
sufentanil. The target concentration of sufentanil
was adjusted and recorded according to mean
arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR) and
bispectral index (BIS). Patients in the TEAS group
received TEAS 30 min before anaesthesia
induction and this was maintained throughout
the operation at four pairs of acupuncture
points. Postoperative pain, recovery and side
effects were evaluated.
Results Eighty-eight patients completed the
study. Continuous monitoring of MAP, HR and
BIS showed stable values with no significant
differences between the two groups (p>0.05).
Sufentanil target plasma concentration in TEAS
patients was significantly lower at some time
points during supratentorial craniotomy, and
total sufentanil consumption was significantly
higher in the sham group (p<0.05).
Postoperative recovery and pain were significantly
improved by TEAS (p<0.001), without the
postoperative side effects.
Conclusions Multipoint TEAS at both proximal
and distal points combined with TIVA can
significantly decrease intraoperative sufentanil
requirements, increase pain relief on
postoperative day 1 and improve postoperative
recovery of patients during supratentorial tumour
resection, with no significant increase of side
effects. These findings suggest that multipoint
TEAS may be clinically effective as an adjunct to
analgesia in intraoperative anaesthesia and
postoperative pain treatment and may speed
recovery.

INTRODUCTION
Acupuncture
is
a
traditional
Chinese
medicine and has been extensively used as
a non-pharmacological analgesic therapy
since being developed 2500 years ago.
Although opioids are widely used as
analgesics
in
general
anaesthesia,
the
unpleasant side effects and delayed post-
operative recovery have set some limita-
tions on their clinical use.1 Several clinical
studies have shown that acupuncture may
be effective in improving postoperative
analgesia, reducing intraoperative anaes-
thetic requirement and immunosuppres-
sion,
and
reducing
the
incidence
of
anaesthesia-related
side
effects.2–5
Stimulation of specific points, by needles
or electrodes, releases neurochemical sub-
strates that may block the incoming pain
information.6 However, several studies
also found that acupuncture and related
techniques did not significantly reduce
pain during the operation,7 and not only

failed to reduce, but actually increased, anaesthetic
requirements.8 These conflicting results may be par-
tially explained by the different acupuncture points
and exogenous anaesthetic used, though other factors,
such as the timing of the intervention and the patients’
level of consciousness, may also be involved.
Transcutaneous
electric
acupuncture
stimulation
(TEAS), or ‘needleless acupuncture’, is a low-skill
alternative to needle-based electroacupuncture (EA)
that combines the advantages of both acupuncture
and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation by
using electrode pads placed on the acupuncture points
instead of piercing the skin with needles.9 Compared
with acupuncture or EA, TEAS is a non-invasive tech-
nique with very low risk of infection and it can poten-
tially be applied by any anaesthetist or preoperative
personnel with minimal training, increasing the repro-
ducibility
of
acupuncture-like
stimulation.
Several
lines of evidence indicate that TEAS can significantly
improve the quality of recovery and decrease the inci-
dence of anaesthesia-related side effects for patients
undergoing surgery.10 However, most clinical studies
have focused on the postoperative pain relief and side
effects of TEAS and, to our knowledge, its intraopera-
tive effects have not been well characterised.
Our previous studies showed that EA at both prox-
imal
and
distal
(peripheral)
acupuncture
points
showed beneficial intraoperative and postoperative
effects on patients undergoing supratentorial tumour
resection.11 EA combined with general anaesthesia sig-
nificantly shortened the anaesthesia recovery time and
decreased both the sevoflurane requirement and post-
operative side effects during resection of a supratentor-
ial tumour.12 TEAS, a therapeutic approach has some
similarities to EA,13 and may also achieve the same
effect
as
EA
in
intraoperative
anaesthesia,
post-
operative analgesia and in the reduction of side effects.
This study was designed to investigate the intrao-
perative and postoperative anaesthetic effects of multi-
point TEAS at both proximal and distal (peripheral)
points during supratentorial craniotomy, when in
combination with sufentanil anaesthesia, including its
effects on intraoperative analgesic requirements, post-
operative pain relief, recovery and improvement in
side effects.
PATIENTS AND METHODS
Patients
This randomised, blind, controlled trial was approved
by the ethics review board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital
and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry
(registration number ChiCTR-TRC-10001078). All
participants
gave
their
written
informed
consent
before being enrolled in this study. A total of 92
patients aged 18–60 years, of American Society of
Anesthesiologists physical status I/II, scheduled for
supratentorial craniotomy, were eligible for this trial
between January 2008 and December 2011. Exclusion
criteria were as follows: women in pregnancy or lacta-
tion; patients with the complication of severe respira-
tory and circulatory system diseases; long-term heavy
smokers; patients with body mass index >35 kg/m2;
emergency patients. Patients with operation time >8 h
and operative blood loss >2500 mL were withdrawn
from the study. Ninety-two patients meeting the cri-
teria were recruited and randomised, but four patients
were withdrawn from the final analysis.
Protocol
Consecutive patients were enrolled and randomly allo-
cated into the TEAS group or sham group according
to a random number table generated by a computer.
No premedication (analgesics and tranquiliser) was
given to any of the patients. In the operation room,
peripheral intravenous infusion was started and non-
invasive arterial blood pressure, heart rate (HR), pulse
oximetry saturation, bispectral index (BIS) and exhal-
ation gas were monitored.
Transcutaneous electrical acupuncture stimulation
Patients in the TEAS group received preoperative
TEAS starting 30 min before anaesthesia induction,
maintained throughout the operation and terminated
at the end of surgery (removal of headstock). Four
pairs of electrodes (3×3 cm2) were applied at five pairs
of acupuncture points: Hegu (LI4) and Waiguan (TE5),
Jinmen (BL63) and Taichong (LR3), Zusanli (ST36)
and Qiuxu (GB 40), and Fengchi (GB20) with Tianzhu
(BL10) and Cuanzhu (BL2) with Yuyao (EX-HN4) on
the craniotomy side. GB20 and BL10, BL2 and
EX-HN4 were adjacent and covered by the same elec-
trode, respectively. TEAS was performed using a Han’s
acupoint nerve stimulator (LH202H, Beijing Huawei
Co, Ltd, Beijing, China) with a dense-disperse fre-
quency of 2/100 Hz (alternated once every 3 s; 0.6 ms
at 2 Hz and 0.2 ms at 100 Hz). The intensity of stimu-
lation was set at 4.89±2.15, 6.79±3.51, 7.04±3.35
and 5.61±2.13, respectively, according to the maximal
tolerance of patients and maintained throughout the
operation. In the sham group, the gel electrodes were
applied in the same therapeutic setting to ensure blind-
ing of the patients, which has previously proved to be
successful.14 The patients were connected to the instru-
ment, but electronic stimulation was not applied. All
the acupuncture-related operations were performed by
the
physicians
of
Beijing
University
of
Chinese
Medicine. Only the acupuncturist was informed of the
randomisation allocation using opaque, sealed envel-
opes, just before the onset of TEAS. Others, including
the anaesthesiologists, surgeons, recovery staff, asses-
sors and participants, were blinded to the group alloca-
tion. Postoperative data were collected by researchers
who were also blinded to the study design.
Anaesthesia
Anaesthesia was induced by intravenous infusion of
propofol (1% Diprivan) and sufentanil (0.2 mg/mL)

to maintain their target plasma concentrations at
5 μg/mL and 0.5 ng/mL, respectively. Sufentanil was
administered as target-controlled infusion using the
pharmacokinetic model of Bovill et al.15 After loss of
consciousness, target plasma concentrations of propo-
fol and sufentanil were each subsequently reduced to
3.2 μg/mL
and
0.3 ng/mL.
Vecuronium
bromide
0.1 mg/kg was given intravenously to facilitate tracheal
intubation and muscle relaxation. Mechanical ventila-
tion with a 40% oxygen/air mixture was applied with
the tidal volume of 10 mL/kg, respiratory frequency
of 12 times/min and gas flow of 1 L/min set.
The dose of propofol was stable during the oper-
ation for anaesthetic maintenance and sufentanil con-
centration was adjusted to maintain the mean arterial
pressure (MAP), HR and BIS in the basic range of
+10% to −20%. Vecuronium bromide of 0.05 mg/kg
was injected intravenously and intermittently to retain
muscle relaxation (T4/T1<25%) according to the
results of muscle relaxation (train of four stimulation).
Local infiltration anaesthesia of the incision was
obtained by 0.5% ropivacaine.
Postoperative analgesia
After the operation, analgesic compound liquid, con-
sisting of 50 mL of normal saline and 100 μg of sufen-
tanil, 1 mL/h was given through a sustaining pump
with a single injecting volume of 0.5 mL and locking
time of 10 min. Patients were asked to rate the degree
of pain on a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) consisting of
a numerical scale from 0 to 10 points; with 0 being
‘no pain’ and 10 being ‘maximum pain’.10
Measurements
The demographic characteristics of the patients were
recorded, as well as the intensity and duration of
TEAS. MAP, HR, BIS and sufentanil target plasma
concentrations were recorded at different stages of the
operation, including preoperation stage (from loss of
consciousness to before scalp cutting), craniotomy
stage (from scalp cutting to dura cutting), intracranial
stage (from after dura opening to before dura stitch-
ing) and cranial closure stage (from dura stitching to
complete scalp stitching).
End points
The primary outcome of this study was the consump-
tion of anaesthetics. The secondary end points were
the time to spontaneous respiration, extubation time,
eye-opening time, time to spontaneous movement,
time to reorientation, and time to discharge from the
operating room. After recovery room admission, the
postoperative
side
effects,
including
incidence
of
respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting and pain,
were also recorded at postoperative days 1, 2 and 3.
Statistical analysis
All data input and the statistical analysis were per-
formed
in
the
department
of
epidemiology and
hygienic
statistics
of
Capital
Medical
University,
Beijing, China, using SAS V.9.0 software (SAS Institute
Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA). The measurement
data with normal distribution are expressed as mean
±SD and were analysed by a two sample t test.
Discrete data were analysed by the χ2 test or Fisher’s
exact probability test, as appropriate. A p value
of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Intraoperative anaesthetics consumption and clinical
recovery from anaesthesia
There were significant differences between the two
groups in the total dose of sufentanil consumption,

of propofol given, eye-opening time, the time to spon-
taneous movement and the time to reorientation were
also found to be reduced or shortened, although dif-
ferences did not reach statistical significance.
Postoperative pain and side effects
No respiratory depression was noted in either group
and the incidences of nausea and vomiting did not
differ significantly between the two groups (table 3).
Pain scores were significantly lower at postoperative
day 1 in the TEAS group than in the sham group,
whereas the VAS score in the TEAS group was
comparatively higher at postoperative days 2 and 3
(figure 2).

DISCUSSION
As a widespread complementary therapy, acupuncture
appears to be effective in the treatment of many symp-
toms and/or disorders, partly by regulating the func-
tions
of
the
autonomic
nervous
system
and
neuroendocrine system,9 especially in improving post-
operative pain relief and reducing postoperative side
effects.16 Given the inherent side effects and limita-
tions of conventional opioid analgesics, increasing
interest has been focused on the use of acupuncture
or acupuncture-related
techniques
in
combination
with general anaesthesia, as well as their effect on
intraoperative anaesthetic and opioid requirements.17
TEAS is an acupuncture-like technique that may
produce effects similar to those elicited by acupunc-
ture or EA treatment.14 In this study, TEAS was used
in coordination with sufentanil to observe the intrao-
perative and postoperative effects of TEAS on patients
undergoing
supratentorial
tumour
resection.
The
results showed that either sufentanil combined with
TEAS or sufentanil alone produced surgical anaesthe-
sia for supratentorial craniotomy. Sufentanil require-
ment, postoperative recovery time and degree of pain
on postoperative day 1 were significantly lower in
patients treated by TEAS than in those without TEAS
(p<0.05 or p<0.01), illustrating clear adjuvant anal-
gesic effects. In contrast, the VAS pain score was
higher in the TEAS group on postoperative days 2
and 3. The explanation for this is unknown and may
relate to the short-term anaesthetic effects of TEAS,
so that the analgesic effect of TEAS may stop after the
operation without further stimulation.18 However, the
postoperative side effects, like vomiting, nausea and
respiratory depression, were not significantly different
between the two groups.
A number of studies have provided evidence that
acupuncture can induce neural responses in different
brain areas.19 20 BL63, LR3, ST36 and GB40 are well-
known analgesia acupuncture points generally used
for pain relief during craniotomy,21 and stimulation at
points LI4 and TE5 has also been used for post-
operative pain relief during clinical surgery.10
14
Corroborating the results of these studies, our previ-
ous studies showed evidence suggesting that EA at
these acupuncture points combined with sevoflurane
anaesthesia during supratentorial tumour resection
significantly reduced the sevoflurane required and the
postoperative side effects and improved the post-
operative recovery from anaesthesia.12 22 EA at both
the aforementioned distal acupuncture points and the
proximal acupuncture points on the craniotomy side,
including GB20, BL10, BL2 and EX-HN4, could
reduce the anaesthetic requirement and improve the
postoperative recovery of patients undergoing supra-
tentorial craniotomy. Alternating stimulation with low
and high frequency (2 and 100 Hz) can stimulate the
release of the three intrinsic opioid peptides (endor-
phin,
enkephalin
and
dynorphin)
in
the
central
nervous
system
to
produce
a
general
analgesic
action.13 Therefore, in this research, four pairs of acu-
puncture points were selected for TEAS treatment.
In our study, propofol was administered for anaes-
thesia maintenance using a target-controlled infusion
system and sufentanil administration was adjusted
according to the arterial pressure, HR and BIS, a well-
established method of monitoring anaesthetic depth
and consequently a useful tool in assessing the risk of
awareness
during
induction
and
maintenance
of
anaesthesia.23 Our results showed that the values of
MAP, HR and BIS remained stable during induction
and maintenance of anaesthesia, with an adequate
level of anaesthesia obtained (BIS was maintained
between 40 and 60 during all operations). There was
no significant difference between the TEAS and sham
groups, and surgical details of patients, like operation
time, anaesthesia time, type of operation, infusion
amount, urine amount, estimated blood loss, were not
significantly
different,
indicating
that
either
the
TEAS-combined with sufentanil or sufentanil alone as
administered
in
our
study
provided
a
clinically
optimal condition for craniotomy. Although the MAP,
HR and BIS values were not statistically significantly
different, MAP in the TEAS group was slightly lower
during
surgery,
while
HR
showed
no
consistent
pattern, BIS was slightly higher during part of the sur-
gical time, unlike the results reported by Si et al.24

TEAS treatment might explain the lower MAP and

Our present study showed that the sufentanil target
plasma concentration was significantly higher in the
sham-treated group during supratentorial craniotomy
surgery and TEAS treatment can help reduce the
intraoperative sufentanil requirement. Meanwhile, the
time to spontaneous respiration, extubation time and
the time to discharge from the operating room were
also found to favour the patient receiving TEAS treat-
ment, in agreement with the results of previous
studies.12 14 The VAS scores were also found to be
reduced by TEAS treatment during postoperative day
1. However, unlike previous studies, which indicated
that TEAS is an effective method for prophylaxis of
postoperative nausea and vomiting,14 we found no
statistically significant difference in the incidence and
severity
of
postoperative
complications,
such
as
respiratory depression, nausea and vomiting. Similar
results were obtained in recent study by Wang et al,14
who hypothesised that this lack of effect may be
attributable to the low incidence of postoperative
nausea and vomiting after intravenous anaesthesia, or
the antiemetics they used. This may not be the case in
our study, where no antiemetic was given, and instead
the different acupuncture points and intensity of
TEAS used may have a role.26 27
In summary, the results of this blinded controlled
study indicated that multipoint TEAS at both proximal
and distal acupuncture points combined with sufenta-
nil anaesthesia can significantly reduce the intraopera-
tive analgesic requirements, strengthen pain relief on
the first postoperative day, and improve the post-
operative recovery of patients undergoing supratentor-
ial tumour resection, although the postoperative side
effects were not found to be significantly improved.
These findings suggest that multipoint TEAS may be
clinically effective as an adjunct to sufentanil in intrao-
perative anaesthesia and postoperative analgesia and
recovery.
