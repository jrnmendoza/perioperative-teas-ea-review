undergoing ESD.
(5) It is reported that 44.9%–62.8% 
of patients suffer moderate-to-severe pain after ESD, 
especially in the early postoperative period (within 
1–4 h after surgery).
(6-8) Postoperative pain not only 
has a direct negative impact on patient satisfaction, 
but also prolongs hospitalization time and increases 
hospitalization costs.
(8,9) However, postoperative pain 
after ESD is always underestimated and ignored by 
anesthesiologists and endoscopists, partly due to the 
emphasis on enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) 
protocol on earlier discharge and earlier mobilization.
(9)
At present, there are no unified strategies on 
how to alleviate postoperative pain and promote 
gastrointestinal function recovery for patients undergoing 
gastroesophageal ESD. In clinical practice, painkillers, 
such as opioids, may mask some postoperative 
complications of ESD, such as perforation, bleeding, 
etc.
(10,11) Some studies have found that intravenous 
injection of dexamethasone or lidocaine, or local 
infusion of bupivacaine and triamcinolone acetonide, 
can alleviate abdominal pain after ESD while reduce 
intraoperative opioid consumption and decrease 
patients' physical activity.
(12-14) Our previous study 
also found that intraoperative dexmedetomidine could 
significantly relieve post-ESD pain and reduce the 
dosage of morphine used after ESD.
(15) However, 
we also found that some patients still experienced 
abdominal distension, gastrointestinal dysfunction, or 
other discomfort.
Inflammatory reactions and burns caused by 
operational stimuli are essential in the development of 
postoperative pain after ESD. Electroacupuncture (EA), 
or transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS) 
is a potential treatment option for postoperative pain, 
nausea, and vomiting. 
(16,17) It can reduce inflammation 
via activating the vagal-adrenal pathway. 
(18) Similarly, we 
reported that TEAS improved gastrointestinal function 
recovery in patients, reduced postoperative pain after 
abdominal surgery, and decreased the concentration 
of brain–gut peptide substance P.
(16) Some systematic 
reviews have reported the promising effects of EA or 
TEAS for postoperative pain managment.
(19,20) However, 
the benefits of TEAS for patients after gastric ESD 
remain unclear. We therefore conducted this prospective, 
randomized clinical trial to assess the efﬁ cacy of TEAS 
for postoperative pain and gastrointestinal function 
recovery in patients after ESD for early gastric and 
esophageal neoplasms.
METHODS
This study was a single-center, prospective, 
randomized, sham-controlled clinical trial conducted 
at Beijing Friendship Hospital affiliated to Capital 
Medical University in China. It was approved by the 
Bioethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, 
Capital Medical University (No. 2021-P2-315-01) and 
registered in the China Clinical Trials Registry (No.
ChiCTR2100052837) on November 6, 2021. The study 
protocol for this trial was published previously.
(21) All 
participants signed the informed consent form after being 
recruited by the anesthesiologist at the preoperative visit.
Participants
This study was performed from December 
2021 to March 2023. Inclusion criteria included: adult 
patients (18–75 years) with American Society of 
Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification 
Ĉ to Ċ, 18 
 body mass index 
 30, and scheduled to 
undergo elective gastric and esophageal ESD. Exclusion 
criteria included: patients who (1) with surgical incision 
or scar on the meridian of the acupoints of Hegu (LI 4), 
Neiguan (PC 6), Shangjuxu (ST 37), or Zusanli (ST 
36); (2) local skin infection at the abovementioned 
acupoints; (3) nerve injury on the upper or lower limbs; 
(4) participated in other clinical trials within the last 
4 weeks; (5) patients do not understand Numeric Rating 
Scale (NRS) score or unable to perform the scoring; 
(6) presence of a cardiac pacemaker; (7) pregnancy or 
in labor; (8) preoperative pain, ongoing use of central 
analgesic medications, and addiction or dependence 
on opioids; (9) severe central nervous system diseases 
or psychiatric disorders that may increase patient's risk 
or interfere with results evaluation.
Randomization and Blinding
Randomization was performed using computer-
generated random numbers with a block size of 4, the 
allocation was sealed in an opaque envelope. Patients 
were randomly assigned to the sham group or TEAS 
group with a ratio of 1:1 by the anesthesiologist. 
Only the anesthesiologist and acupuncturist who 
was in charge of TEAS stimulation were aware of 
the grouping, the patients, surgeons, and physicians 
responsible for follow-up were unaware.
Intervention
Both TEAS and sham stimulation were performed

by a licensed acupuncturist with more than 3 years 
of practice. Patients in both groups received the 
intervention from 30 min before ESD until the end of 
surgery.
Patients in the TEAS group were treated bilaterally 
at the LI 4 and PC 6, ST 36 and ST 37. The detail of the 
location of acupoints are presented in the Appendix 1. 
Self-adhesive electrodes with wires were attached to 
the location of these 4 acupoints and connected to the 
HANS acupoint nerve stimulator (HANS-200A, Nanjing 
Jisheng Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China), with 
the current frequency of 2/100 Hz (wave width: 2 Hz, 
0.6 ms; 100 Hz, 0.2 ms), alternating frequency for 3 s, 
and stimulation intensity was set to the patient's 
maximum tolerance level. In the sham group, 2 of the 
sham points were located at 7 cun above and 1 cun 
outside Shenmen (HT 7). The other 2 sham points were 
at 9 cun and 12 cun above Kunlun (BL 60, Appendix 2). 
Self-adhesive electrodes were glued to these points, 
but no electrical stimulation was given from the HANS 
stimulator. 
Anesthesia Procedures
On the day before ESD, all enrolled patients signed 
informed consent and received relevant introduction on 
the implementation and follow-up. Electrocardiogram, 
blood pressure, pulse oxygen saturation, and bispectral 
(BIS) were monitored before anesthesia. TEAS and 
sham stimulation were conducted by the experienced 
acupuncturist 30 min before ESD. Midazolam (5 mg, 
0.03 mg/kg; Jiangsu Ehwa Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., 
TMD25B01), remifentanil (1 mg, 1–2 «g/kg; Yichang 
Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., AD5030381), etomidate 
(20 mg, 0.1–0.2 mg/kg; Jiangsu Ehwa Pharmaceutical 
Co., Ltd., TYT24K18) and rocuronium (25 mg, 
0.6–0.8 mg/kg; Hainan STAR Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., 
2412045A) were used before intubation. Anesthesia 
was maintained with a total intravenous infusion of 
propofol [200 mg, 4–6 mg/(kg•h); Beijing Fresenius 
Kabi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., 16TK1957], remifentanil 
[0.05–0.2 «g/(kg•min)], and rocuronium (one-third of 
the induced dose every 40 min). Anesthetic depth was 
controlled by the adjustment of intravenous propofol to 
maintain a BIS index of 45–55 during the procedure. A 
total of 50 mg tramadol (100 mg, Grunenthal GmbH, 
00406X) was applied for analgesia 30 min before the 
end of ESD. At the end of the surgery, the patients were 
extubated and transferred to the post-anesthesia care 
unit in routine.
NRS was used to assess the patient's pain level. 
If the NRS score 
 4 points or the patient requested 
analgesia during the follow-up, 1 mg morphine 
(Northeast Pharmaceutical Group, Shenyang First 
Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., 241103) was administered 
intravenously. Intraoperative mean arterial pressure 
(MAP), heart rate (HR), SpO2, dose of drugs as well as 
ﬂ uid input and output were recorded. The concomitant 
medication during the trial period from the start of 
surgery to 2 days after the end was recorded.
Primary and Secondary Outcomes
A trained research assessor who blinded to the 
group allocation performed the assessment of primary 
and secondary outcomes. The primary outcome was 
the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain evaluated 
by NRS score within 24 h postoperative period. The 
scale was 0–10: 1–3 for mild pain, 4–6 for moderate 
pain, and 7–10 for severe pain. The moderate-to-
severe post-ESD pain was deﬁ ned as NRS 
 4.
(15,16) 
The secondary outcomes included the NRS pain 
scores at 1, 2, 4, 6, 18, 24, and 48 h postoperatively; 
the consumption of morphine, gastrointestinal 
function recovery time, the incidence of postoperative 
complications, and patient satisfaction during follow-up 
period. A scale with a maximum score of 100 was 
utilized to evaluate patient satisfaction after ESD. Zero 
point indicated extreme dissatisfaction, and 100 points 
indicate very satisfaction, with patients scoring verbally.
For recovery of gastrointestinal function, we mainly 
recorded the time of ﬁ rst ﬂ atus, defecation, drink, and solid 
food intake of patients. The main recorded postoperative 
complications were hypertension, hypotension, 
tachycardia, bradycardia, postoperative nausea and 
vomiting (PONV), gastrointestinal perforation or bleeding, 
reoperation, and fever. Postoperative AEs were recorded 
by the follow-up anesthesiologist, and in the event of a 
malignant event such as bleeding >500 mL, the trial will 
be immediately terminated and reported to the principal 
investigator and a review board will be established.

moderate-to-severe pain after ESD was 64%. We 
hypothesized that TEAS could reduce the incidence

RESULTS
Patients and Surgery Characteristics
From December 2021 to March 2023, a total 
of 275 patients were assessed for eligibility. Among 
them, 11 patients did not meet the inclusion criteria, 
3 had ASA Physical Status Classiﬁ cation grades > Ċ, 
125 had took part in earlier research, 2 with cardiac 
pacemakers, and 5 had opted out of the trial. As a result, 
129 patients were enrolled and randomized assigned 
to the TEAS or the sham group. One patient from the 
TEAS group and 1 from the sham group were converted 
to laparotomy due to further pathological infiltration 
of lesions and were eliminated from the study. Two 
patients from the TEAS group and 1 from the sham 
group dropped out due to postoperative bleeding. Three 
patients from the TEAS group and 1 from the sham 
group withdrew from the trial. Finally, a total of 120 
patients completed the entire research, 58 in the TEAS 
group and 62 in the sham group (Figure 1).

Based on recently published data, the incidence 
of moderate-to-severe pain induced by ESD was as

obtained in Jung's study.
(22) In our research, we found

sham group was 64.5%. It was further demonstrated 
that the rate of post-ESD pain is high enough for 
physicians to pay more attention and make active pain 
management. However, there are currently no unified 
measure on how to carry out good postoperative 
analgesia for patients undergoing gastroesophageal 
ESD. Our study found that TEAS can effectively alleviate 
post-ESD pain. In this regard, several recently published 
investigations conﬁ rmed that TEAS can provide powerful 
protection against postoperative pain for a variety of 
procedures, like gastric and colorectal surgery
(16) as well 
as pediatric orthopedic surgery.
(23) More importantly, we 
also found that pretreating with TEAS was a protective 
factor associated with reduced incidence of moderate-to-
severe pain according to logistic regression analysis.
Based on published data, post-ESD pain is more 
severe in the early postoperative period (within 1–4 h 
after surgery).
(6-8) In fact, this was also conﬁ rmed in our 
preliminary experiment.
(21) In line with these results, 
we followed up the NRS scores of patients in the early 
postoperative period. Moreover, we appropriately 
extended the visit to 48 h after surgery. Further 
investigation showed that TEAS could improve post-
ESD pain within 24 h after surgery. From the results of 
the distribution gragh of NRS, at 1 h after surgery, we 
found that TEAS reduced the percentage of patients 
with moderate pain to 12.1%, while the sham group 
was 48.4%. At 2, 4 and 6 h after ESD, the patients 
with moderate pain in both groups were reduced, but 
the proportion of patients with moderate pain in the 
TEAS group was lower than that in the sham group. 
This result was exactly corresponding to the patients 
who were given morphine treatment, because at the 
beginning of the trial design, patients with moderate 
pain would be given morphine treatment. Except for 
some patients who refused to use morphine, most 
patients received morphine treatment when NRS >4 
for the ﬁ rst time, and then the pain was relieved. TEAS 
decreased the percentage of NRS >4 at 18 and 24 h. 
At 48 h post-ESD, the difference was not signiﬁ cant. 
This result revealed that the adjuvant analgesic effect 
of perioperative TEAS could last until 24 h after ESD.
In addition, a recently published retrospective 
study confirmed that gender, operation duration, and 
lesion site were inﬂ uence factors affecting postoperative 
pain in ESD.
(8) Strikingly, our study revealed that patients 
undergoing gastric ESD had a much lower incidence 
of moderate-to-severe pain than esophageal ESD. 
This may be due to the different mechanisms of pain 
transmission in different organs. Moreover, we cannot 
rule out the possibility that increased peristalsis at the 
esophageal site and frequent contractions may also lead 
to aggravated postoperative pain after ESD.
In this study, peri-operative TEAS treatment 
decreased the cases of PONV, this result is similar to 
our previous study in gastrointestinal surgery.
(16) As 
known, LI 4, PC 6, ST 36, and ST 37 are the most 
commonly used acupoints for regulating the intestines 
and stomach, channeling the channels and collaterals. 
It was well documented that stimulating these acupoints 
could improve intestinal motility, and regulate endocrine 
function, which helps reduce PONV.
(24) However, 
the data from this study failed to confirm that TEAS 
improved gastrointestinal function in patients after 
gastroesophageal ESD. This result is inconsistent with 
another study by our team.
(16) In this regard, we consider 
that ESD is a minimally invasive surgery in nature, it has 
little interference with gastrointestinal function, so it has 
almost no impact on the exhaust function of patients. 
What is more, the eating and drinking time of patients 
after ESD surgery is basically carried out according to 
the doctor's advice, the time of all patients is therefore 
relatively ﬁ xed.
There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, 
TEAS was performed only during the operation, and 
there was no postoperative management. Although, in 
our previous observation, we found that the postoperative 
pain was most severe in the early postoperative period 
for most patients. Hence, TEAS was not immediate 
administered after patients returned to the ward. However, 
we still cannot rule out that this may be related to the lack 
of difference in pain 1 h after ESD between the TEAS and 
the sham groups. Secondly, some analgesic substances 
such as endorphins, or some injury-related factors such as 
interleukin-6 to quantitatively compare the postoperative 
pain response between the two groups were not included 
in the study due to limited research fund.
In conclusion, data from this study showed that 
pretreating with TEAS could effectively improve the 
early postoperative pain of patients undergoing ESD, 
and reduce the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain, 
providing a feasible strategy for relieving the condition.
