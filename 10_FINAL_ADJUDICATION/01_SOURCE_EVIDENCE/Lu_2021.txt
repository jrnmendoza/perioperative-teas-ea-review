A B S T R A C T 
Study objective: Despite multiple interventions, the incidence of chronic pain after mastectomy could be as high as 
50% after surgery. This study aimed to determine the efficacy of transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation 
(TEAS) before anesthesia induction in reducing chronic pain and to compare the effect of combined acupoint 
TEAS with that of single acupoint TEAS. 
Design: A multicenter randomized clinical trial. 
Setting: The study was conducted at six medical centers in China from May 2016 to April 2018. Final follow-up 
was on October 26, 2018. 
Participants: Eligible patients were women scheduled for radical mastectomy under general anesthesia. 
Interventions: Patients were randomly and equally grouped into sham control (n = 188), single acupoint (PC6, n 
= 198), or combined acupoints (PC6 and CV17, n = 190) TEAS groups using a centralized computer-generated 
randomization system. TEAS was applied for 30 min before anesthesia induction. The sham-operated control 
group received electrode attachment but without stimulation. Anesthesiologists, surgeons, and outcome assessors 
were blinded to the interventions. 
Measures: The primary endpoint was the incidence of chronic pain 6 months after surgery. Incidences were 
compared among the groups using the unadjusted χ2 test.

mastectomy pain at 6 months was reported in 42 of 190 patients (22.1%) in the combined acupoints group, 
65 of 188 patients (34.6%) in the sham-operated group (P = 0.007; relative risk [RR], 95% confidence interval 
[CI]: 0.68, 0.52–0.89), and 72 of 198 patients (36.4%) in the single acupoint group (P = 0.002; RR, 95% CI: 0.72,

1. Introduction 
Persistent postsurgical pain is clinically relevant in patients under­
going mastectomy, which causes both physical discomfort and daily 
dysfunction. The compromised daily function of the patients affects the 
entire society to operate effectively and economically [1]. In a recent 
study by Sessler et al. [2], incision pain at 6 months after mastectomy 
and chronic persistent neuropathic pain was reported in 52% and 10% of 
the patients, respectively. At least 50% of patients had mild pain, and 
16% had moderate to severe pain, 1 year after lumpectomy and mas­
tectomy [3]. 
Current treatments for persistent pain after mastectomy include 
gabapentinoids, antidepressants, regional nerve block, laser therapy, 
and thermal radiofrequency of the stellate ganglion [4]; however, no 
cure has been guaranteed. Previous studies demonstrated that acute 
pain and anesthesia management is closely associated with the devel­
opment of chronic pain [5,6]. We hypothesized that adequate man­
agement of acute pain and anesthesia might be a suitable target to 
reduce chronic pain after mastectomy. Acupuncture and related tech­
niques such as transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS) 
have been shown to reduce the consumption of opioids during general 
anesthesia [7,8] and are used to treat acute post-surgical pain [9,10]. 
Therefore, this multicenter, prospective, randomized, controlled trial 
aimed to investigate the effect of TEAS before anesthesia induction on 
post-mastectomy chronic pain and to compare the efficacy of combined 
acupoint TEAS with that of single acupoint TEAS. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Study overview 
The study was conducted at six medical centers and was approved by 
the institutional review boards of Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military 
Medical University; First Affiliated Hospital, Xi'an Jiaotong University; 
Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University; First Affiliated 
Hospital, Zhengzhou University; First Affiliated Hospital, Chongqing 
Medical University; and People's Hospital of Zhengzhou University. It 
was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02741726). Written informed 
consent was obtained from each participant before the study. The pro­
tocol adherence of the six participating centers was assessed by an in­
dependent observer through regular visits. The detailed protocol of this 
clinical trial is also submitted as a supplemental file. 
2.2. Patient characteristics 
Women aged 18–65 years who were scheduled for elective radical 
mastectomy under general anesthesia were recruited for this study. 
Exclusion criteria included contraindications to TEAS (scar or skin 
damage at the stimulation site, electronic devices such as pacemakers or 
other implanted medical electronic devices in the body); communication 
difficulties; history of breast surgery; drug abuse/addiction; or alcohol 
abuse or addiction; cardiac dysfunction or severe hypertension; 
confirmed liver/renal dysfunction; giving consent for another inter­
ventional study; or declined participation. 
2.3. Randomization and masking 
Study participants were randomized 1:1:1 into the sham-operated, 
single acupoint (TEAS at PC6), and combined acupoint groups (TEAS 
at PC6 and CV17) using a secured web-based system that was stratified 
according to permuted blocks (random block sizes; maximum of 12) by 
the treatment center. Outcomes were assessed by trained investigators 
who were blinded to the treatment allocation. The patients and in­
vestigators who participated in the intervention were not masked. Other 
clinical staff, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, and ward staff, were 
unaware of the allocation. 
2.4. Study intervention 
Transcutaneous acupoint stimulation was performed in compliance 
with recommendations from the Standards for Reporting of Controlled 
Trials in Acupuncture [11]. Briefly, electrodes were attached to the skin 
surface of the acupoint and connected to the Hwato Electric Acupunc­
ture Treatment Instrument by leads (model No. SDZ-V; Suzhou Medical 
Appliances Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China; see Supplemental Material 1). Pa­
tients in the combined acupoints group received TEAS at bilateral PC6 
(Neiguan, a key acupoint of the hand-jueyin pericardium meridian) and 
CV17 (Danzhong, a key acupoint of the Ren meridian). Patients in the 
single acupoint group received TEAS at the bilateral PC6 acupoints (see 
Supplemental Material 1). Patients in the sham-operated group received 
electrode attachment but without stimulation. The device provided 
“disperse-dense” waves with alternating frequencies of 2 Hz and 10 Hz 
for two cycles. The stimulation intensity was identified as the maximal 
tolerance to the “Teh Chi” sensations of heaviness, numbness, and 
swelling at the point of stimulation. Anesthesia induction was initiated 
after 30 min of TEAS. 
Interventions were performed by a designated investigator who was 
not involved in the anesthesia or follow-up. The stimulator was placed in 
an opaque box to blind the surgical team and anesthesiologist for group 
allocation. 
2.5. Patient-reported outcome measures 
At 3 months and 6 months after surgery, the patients were followed 
up using a telephonic questionnaire [6]. The primary endpoint was the 
incidence of post-mastectomy chronic pain at 6 months after surgery, 
which was defined as chronic pain in the breast scar area, axilla, arm, 
and side of the body. The questionnaire is provided in Supplemental 
Material 2. 
The secondary endpoints included remifentanil consumption during 
general anesthesia, the time to the first verbal response and the time to 
endotracheal extubation, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), 
respiratory depression, numeric rating scale (NRS) scores, and demand 
for rescue analgesics, patient satisfaction scores on analgesia by 24 h 
after surgery, and the incidence of chronic pain at 3 months after sur­
gery. In case of pain at 3 months or 6 months after surgery, the severity 
of chronic pain was assessed using the NRS [6,12]. 
2.6. Procedures 
All surgical procedures were performed under total intravenous 
anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Anesthesia was induced with 
midazolam, propofol, and fentanyl, and continuous infusion of remi­
fentanil and propofol was titrated to maintain a Narcotrend index of 
40–60 and stable hemodynamics. After endotracheal extubation, the 
patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit. Forty

Sufentanil was used for patient-controlled analgesia after surgery. Par­

2.7. Statistical analysis 
The primary hypothesis was that TEAS would reduce chronic pain at 
6 months after mastectomy, and combined acupoint stimulation would 
be more effective than single acupoint stimulation. We calculated that a 
sample size of 537 participants would have 80% power to detect a dif­
ference in the incidence of chronic pain at 6 months after mastectomy 
among the combined acupoints (25%), single acupoint group (30%), and 
sham-operated groups (40%), using two degrees of freedom chi-square 
test with a significance alpha level of 0.05. Considering a dropout rate 
of 5%, we defined a sample size of 576 participants. 
We performed all the analyses in an intention-to-treat population, 
which included all patients who had undergone randomization and in­
terventions. Descriptive statistics were applied to present the charac­
teristics of each group with mean ± standard deviation, median 
(interquartile), or the number of cases (%). Either analysis of variance or 
Chi-square test was used to reveal the baseline balance between groups, 
depending on the characteristics of the variables. The incidence of post- 
mastectomy chronic pain at 3 months and 6 months after surgery was 
compared with an unadjusted chi-square test. Logistic regression models 
with log links to estimate relative risk (RR) were used to assess RR with a 
95% confidence interval (CI). Multiple comparisons between two groups 
were performed using the Chi-square partition method, in which the 
type I error was corrected to 0.0125 by the Bonferroni-adjusted method. 
Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P-value of 0.05. In a post- 
hoc analysis, we assessed the heterogeneity of the treatment effect on 
post-mastectomy chronic pain at 6 months after surgery across study 
sites by testing for the treatment-by-site interaction. An interaction term 
<0.10 suggested potential treatment effect heterogeneity across sites. 
For missing data, analysis was performed according to a worst-case 
scenario. In particular, patients lost to follow-up in the two TEAS 
groups and those in the sham group were considered with and without 
pain, respectively. Stratified analysis by age, pre-existing pain at the 
surgery site, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy were 
performed using the Cochran-Mantel-Haensel test. All statistical ana­
lyses were conducted using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.2 
(SAS Institute Inc., 2010). 
3. Results 
3.1. Patient characteristics 
From May 2016 to April 2018, 1593 patients were screened in six 
medical centers (Fig. 1). A total of 576 patients met the criteria and were 
randomly assigned into the sham-operated control (n = 188), single 
acupoint (n = 198), and combined acupoints (n = 190) groups (Fig. 1). 
Four patients discontinued the intervention owing to a change in the 
surgery. Another four patients were lost to follow-up. Therefore, 568 
patients were included in the study. No significant difference was found

characteristics, anesthetic and surgical parameters, and postoperative 
treatments did not differ among the groups (Table 1). Patient charac­

3.2. Primary outcome 
In the intention-to-treat analysis, post-mastectomy chronic pain at 6 
months after surgery was reported in 42 of 190 patients (22.1%) in the 
combined acupoints group, which was significantly lower than that in 
65 of 188 patients (34.6%) in the sham-operated group (P = 0.001; RR, 
95% CI: 0.68, 0.52–0.89) and 79 of 196 patients (40.3%) in the single 
acupoint group (P = 0.001; RR, 95% CI: 0.72, 0.55–0.93). The treatment 
effects were homogeneous across the trial sites. Analysis of the primary 
outcome stratified by age, pre-existing pain at the surgery site, chemo­
therapy, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy showed no influence of 
these factors (Supplemental Material 4). However, the NRS score of pain 
provided by patients who reported pain at 6 months after surgery did not 
differ among the groups (P = 0.31) (Table 2). 
3.3. Secondary outcomes 
Remifentanil consumption during surgery was significantly lower in 
the combined acupoints group than in the sham group (Table 3, P <
0.001, group difference 95% CI: −0.21 [−0.34, −0.07]); however, it was 
not different between the single acupoint and sham groups (P = 0.04). 
The time to first verbal response and endotracheal extubation in the 
combined acupoints and single acupoint groups did not differ compared 
to the sham control group (P = 0.80 and 0.82, respectively). The 
incidence of PONV at 24 h after surgery was 18.4% in the combined 
acupoints group, which was significantly lower than that in the single 
acupoint group (29.8%, P = 0.004; RR, 95% CI: 0.71 [0.53, 0.94]) and 
sham group (36.2%, P < 0.001; RR, 95% CI: 0.60 [0.45, 0.81]). The 
severity score of PONV in the combined acupoints group was lower than 
that in both the single acupoint and sham groups (P < 0.001). However, 
further analysis revealed that the incidence of nausea differed among 
the groups (P < 0.001), but not the incidence of vomiting (P = 0.93). The 
NRS scores at rest and cough did not differ among the three groups (P =
0.12 and 0.49). The demand for rescue analgesics and patient satisfac­
tion score for analgesia were similar among the groups (P = 0.58) 
(Table 3). 
Post-mastectomy pain at 3 months after surgery was reported in 58 of 
190 patients (30.5%) in the combined acupoints group, 75 of 188 pa­
tients (39.9%) in the sham group, and 70 of 198 patients (35.4%) in the 
single acupoint group (Table 2). There were no significant differences 
among the three groups. However, for patients with chronic pain at 3 
months post-surgery, the NRS scores showed a significant difference (P 
= 0.04). Pairwise comparison showed that patients in the combined 
acupoints group had a lower NRS score at 3 months after surgery than 
that in the sham-operated group (P = 0.03, mean difference, 95% CI: 
−0.56 [−1.01, 0.11]). At 6 months after surgery, patients with chronic 
pain in the three groups had similar NRS scores. Moreover, there was no 
difference in the number of patients with the neuropathic component, 
disability of the ipsilateral limb or shoulder, the problem of self-care and 
usual activities, or seeking medical management of pain among the 
three groups both at 3 months and 6 months after surgery. The results of 
the per-protocol analysis did not differ from those of the intention-to- 
treat analyses (Supplemental Material 3). 
3.4. Adverse events 
One patient in the single acupoint group reported discomfort in the 
skin area attached to the electrodes. No differences were found among 
the three groups. 
4. Discussion 
This multicenter, prospective, randomized, controlled trial revealed 
that TEAS at combined acupoints before anesthesia induction in patients 
undergoing mastectomy is associated with a decreased incidence of 
chronic pain at 6 months after surgery compared with the sham inter­
vention. This discovery may have potential clinical significance due to 
high prevalence of post-mastectomy chronic pain. 
In our study, the incidence of chronic pain at 6 months after mas­
tectomy was 34.6% in the sham-operated group. The incidence of post- 
mastectomy chronic pain varies in previous studies, depending on the 
population, surgical technique, and assessment methods. Postoperative 
chronic pain has been managed similar to acute pain, with inadequate 
analgesia for acute pain being a risk factor [13–16]. Furthermore, higher 
morphine consumption 24 h post-surgery was observed in patients with 
increased incidence and severity of chronic pain [6]. Due to the side 
effects of opioids, combined analgesia using non-opioids for acute pain 
management is a fascinating strategy. The observed efficacy of TEAS on 
post-mastectomy chronic pain highlighted its possible role in acute pain 
management before surgery for reducing chronic pain after surgery. The 
lower incidence of post-mastectomy chronic pain by pre-surgery TEAS 
observed in the present study could be related to the TEAS-induced 
opioid-sparing effect. Previous studies have reported that avoiding 
high-dose exposure to the short-acting opioid remifentanil can be 
important for the prevention of postoperative chronic pain [16–18]. 
These results indicate that TEAS applied before surgery may be benefi­
cial for the long-term efficacy of pain prevention. Acupuncture has also 
been used for the treatment of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neu­
ropathy symptoms and joint pain related to aromatase inhibitors 
[10,19].

carrageenan-induced inflammatory pain [27,29,30]. In previous studies 
on acupuncture analgesia, both 2/10 Hz and 2/100 Hz protocols were 
frequently used [31]. In our previous studies, we used the 2/10 Hz 
protocol and found it to be effective for reducing opioid consumption in 
patients undergoing sinusotomy and ambulatory breast surgery. 
Therefore, in this study, we followed the stimulation protocol described 
in our previous studies. In the present study, we did not measure the 
levels of endogenous opioids. Further studies are needed to investigate 
the underlying mechanism of acupuncture before surgery to decrease 
postoperative chronic pain. 
Several other risk factors, such as age, axillary surgery, radiotherapy, 
body mass index, preoperative pain at the surgical site, nerve damage, 
psychosocial factors, and genetic predisposition have been proposed to 
influence chronic pain after mastectomy [15]. In our study, baseline 
characteristics including age, surgical technique, pre-existing pain at the 
surgical site, and adjuvant therapies such as chemotherapy, radio­
therapy, and endocrine therapy were not different among the groups. 
Previous studies have shown that approximately 14% of patients 
experience long-term moderate-to-severe pain in the area of breast 
surgery [15]. In our study, we found a lower incidence of chronic pain at 
6 months after mastectomy in the combined-acupoint TEAS group 
(22.1%) than that in the sham (34.6%) and single-acupoint TEAS groups 
(36.4%). We only compared the NRS scores of patients with chronic pain 
and found no differences among the groups. Although the NRS scores of 
patients with chronic pain at 3 months and 6 months after surgery were 
low (around 2) in all the groups, the pain-affected the movement of the 
ipsilateral limb or shoulder (Table 2). 
There is no consensus on the number of acupoints that should be 
selected in acupuncture therapy. In real-world practice, acupuncturists 
decide the number of acupoints based on the theory of traditional Chi­
nese medicine and their personal experiences. In the present study, we 
designed a protocol to compare the efficacy of TEAS between a single 
acupoint and combined acupoints to clarify whether TEAS at a single 
acupoint is sufficient to induce analgesic efficacy for long-term chronic 
pain after surgery. Unlike the efficacy of combined acupoint stimulation, 
although single acupoint TEAS could decrease remifentanil consump­
tion during surgery, it could not reduce the NRS score and incidence of 
chronic pain by 3 or 6 months after surgery. Our previous clinical trial 
found that TEAS with two acupoints showed a stronger sparing effect of 
sufentanil than that with a single acupoint during cardiac surgery under 
general anesthesia [32]. A limited number of clinical trials have sug­
gested a better efficacy of acupuncture using acupoint combinations 
than using a single acupoint in the treatment of various diseases. In rats, 
Yan et al. found that EA at CV12 (Zhongwan), ST25 (Tianshu), and ST37 
(Shangjuxu) were better at relieving acetic acid-induced intestinal 
mucosal lesions than EA at a single acupoint [33]. A study assessing 
PONV found that double-acupoint stimulation was better than single 
acupoint stimulation [34]. Furthermore, acupuncture on DU26 (Shui­
gou) and KI1 (Yongquan) accelerates the recovery of consciousness after 
general anesthesia, with a possible synergistic effect of DU26 and KI1 
[35]. In the present study, P6 and RN17 were selected for combined 
acupoint stimulation. P6 is a frequently used acupoint for analgesia 
[30]. RN17 is the key acupoint of the Ren meridian. According to 
traditional Chinese medicine theory, RN17 is important for treating 
chest diseases, including those of the breast. Combined TEAS of P6 and 
RN17 significantly decreased pain and PONV compared to the sham 
intervention and single acupoint stimulation, indicating the necessity of 
using multiple acupoints in TEAS. 
Imaging studies also provide evidence that suggest the use of com­
bined acupoints [36,37]. By measuring functional brain activities, brain 
regions that responded to acupuncture at different acupoints were 
identified. Using functional resting-state magnetic resonance imaging, 
Zhang et al. observed an increased number of brain regions with changes 
in their activities and stronger activation after performing acupuncture 
at the LR3 (Taichong) and KI3 (Taixi) acupoints compared with per­
forming acupuncture at LR3 alone [38]. An observational study found 
that general anesthesia in combination with triple-acupoint TEAS during 
radical mastectomy significantly decreased the demand for analgesics 
and improved acute pain relief. An increased level of plasma beta- 
endorphin was observed, which might be one of the underlying mech­
anisms [39]. 
4.1. Limitations of the study 
This study had some limitations. First, TEAS before anesthesia in­
duction could not be administered in a patient-blinded manner. The lack 
of blinding may have introduced bias to some extent in outcome 
monitoring. However, the investigators who performed the outcome 
assessment were blinded to group allocation. The stimulator was placed 
in an opaque box so that the surgeons and anesthesiologists were blin­
ded. Electrodes were attached, and the threshold for stimulation was 
tested for all patients. Each patient was informed regarding the possible 
stimulation during TEAS. These procedures may minimize bias. Second, 
we excluded patients older than 65 years, which might have affected the 
generalizability of our results. Age has been significantly associated with 
chronic pain after breast surgery; young age is associated with higher 
risk. Patients aged 18–39 years and those aged 40–49 years have the 
highest risk of pain after breast conservative surgery and mastectomy, 
respectively [14]. Thus, our results indicate that patients with a higher 
risk of chronic pain may benefit from acupoint stimulation. Third, we 
did not compare the effects of acupoint stimulation with that of non- 
acupoint stimulation, owing to our previous study findings in which 
we verified that non-acupoint stimulation could not induce a 
remifentanil-sparing effect in patients undergoing sinusotomy [7]. The 
specificity of acupoints has been proven in another previous study [40]. 
5. Conclusion 
In patients undergoing radical mastectomy under general anesthesia, 
TEAS at combined acupoints before anesthesia induction is associated 
with less chronic pain 6 months after surgery than sham intervention.
