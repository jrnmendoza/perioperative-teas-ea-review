Effects of perioperative electroacupuncture on postoperative 
gastrointestinal recovery after thoracoscopic lung surgery 
Jie Yang a, Libing Huang a,*, Juan Zhu a, Siying Liu a, Fangbing Ji a, Weiqian Tian a, Zhen Zheng b, 
Man Zheng a,* 
a Department of Anesthesiology, Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Hanzhong Road 155, Nanjing 210029, China 
b Discipline of Chinese Medicine, School of Health and Biomedical Sciences, RMIT University, PO BOX 71, Bundoora, VIC 3083, Australia 
A R T I C L E I N F O 
Keywords: 
Electroacupuncture 
Postoperative ileus 
Enhanced recovery 
Thoracic surgery 
A B S T R A C T 
Objective: To study whether perioperative electroacupuncture (EA) can improve postoperative gastrointestinal 
recovery in patients receiving thoracoscopic lung surgery. 
Methods: This study was a single-center, prospective, randomized open-label trial. 180 patients who underwent 
video-assisted thoracoscopic segmentectomy or lobectomy were randomized to EA group (three sessions, 24 h 
prior to surgery, postoperative 4 h and 24 h) or usual care group (UC group). The primary outcomes were time to 
first flatus and defecation. Secondary outcomes included incidence and degree of abdominal distention, post­
operative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and pain scores within 72 h after surgery, postoperative morphine use, 
time to ambulation, and length of hospital stay. 
Results: Time to first flatus (15.4 ± 3.2 h vs. 17.0 ± 3.7 h, P = 0.004) and time to first defecation (75.9 ± 7.9 vs. 
79.7 ± 8.1 h, P = 0.002) in the EA group were significantly shorter than the UC group. The incidences of 
abdominal distension and PONV postoperative 24 h were significantly reduced in the EA group (P < 0.05). There 
was no difference in postoperative pain intensity, morphine use, time to ambulation, and length of hospital stay 
between the two groups (P>0.05). 
Conclusion: Electroacupuncture is a simple intervention for accelerating postoperative gastrointestinal recovery 
and may be considered as an adjunct strategy in enhanced recovery protocols in thoracoscopic lung surgery. 
Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) combined with the 
Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS) has been shown to be associ­
ated with short hospital stay and a quick and safe return to normal ac­
tivity1. Opioid sparing multimodal analgesia, minimally invasive 
surgery, fewer tubes and intravenous lines are important components of 
enhanced recovery protocols. Despite these efforts, many patients still 
suffer transient impairment of gastrointestinal motility after thoracic 
surgery, which compromises postoperative recovery2. Nearly one third 
of patients reported that their daily life was affected by constipation 
after discharge, and constipation is often an overlooked aspect of patient 
care3. In our previous follow-up, gastrointestinal dysfunction after 
thoracic surgery was common, which was uncomfortable for patients, 
and affected postoperative quality of life4. From this perspective, 
reducing duration of gastrointestinal dysfunction is regarded as an 
essential part of the ERAS protocols. 
Postoperative ileus (POI), as a clinical manifestation of impairment 
in gastrointestinal motility following surgery, is characterized by pain, 
transient postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), inability to pass 
flatus or stool and abdominal distension5–7. POI is highly inconvenient, 
embarrassing and uncomfortable for patients, and is closely related to 
poor quality of life and increased healthcare cost6,7. A retrospective 
study conducted on participants undergoing thoracic surgery showed 
that more than 25 % of participants suffered from varied levels of 
postoperative constipation within 30 days after surgery2. Delayed 
gastrointestinal motility was observed both on the 1st and 3rd post­
operative days regardless of the postoperative analgesic technique 
used2. The precise mechanism and cause of POI remains unclear. Factors 
associated with the development of POI may include inflammation 
response induced by surgery and anesthesia, long duration of surgery, 
hemorrhage, activated sympathetic tone, systemic opioid analgesia, 
mental status and bowel edema5,8. Furthermore, changes in normal diet, 
less immobilization and unfamiliarity with surroundings can also lead to 
changes in defecation patterns3. Due to the complex etiological factors 
of POI, there remains room for improvement in the prevention and

treatment of POI. 
Many strategies have been proposed to improve POI, such as pre­
operative carbohydrate loading, early postoperative feeding, fluid re­
striction and minimally invasive surgery, neostigmine, laxatives, 
erythromycin, naloxone, metoclopramide, chewing gum and prokinetic 
agents9,10. However, none offer a convincing benefit, nor are they free 
from side effects. Acupuncture, a well-accepted potential alternative 
therapy, can offer certain beneficial effects, such as alleviating preop­
erative anxiety and reducing postoperative pain and postoperative 
nausea and vomiting (PONV) 11–13. Electroacupuncture (EA), which is 
adding electrical stimulation to the common manual needling, has 
recently been used as a treatment option for accelerating the recovery of 
gastrointestinal function after several types of abdominal surgery14–16. 
Systemic review concluded that EA was a safe and effective treatment 
for POI after abdominal surgery17. 
Few well randomized trials have assessed the effectiveness of EA on 
gastrointestinal function in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery. 
This study aimed to evaluate the effect of EA on gastrointestinal re­
covery in patients who underwent minimally invasive thoracic lung 
surgery. 
Patients and methods 
This study was approved and audited by Ethics Committee of Jiangsu 
Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine, and was registered in the Chi­
nese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900024840). A single-center, 
prospective, randomized study was conducted on patients who under­
went thoracoscopic lung surgery from September 1, 2019 to October 20, 
2020. Patients were provided by the principal investigator with details 
regarding the assessment procedure, and gave informed written consent 
before their scheduled surgery. 
Selection criteria 
Inclusion criteria were patients scheduled for elective video-assisted 
thoracoscopic lobectomy or segmental resection surgery, age older than 
18 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists grade I–II, and will­
ingness to sign the informed consent. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 
patients with severe systemic disease, such as cardiovascular, hepatic, or 
renal abnormalities before operation; patients with cognitive dysfunc­
tion, psychological disorder; history of prior esophageal or gastric sur­
gery; history of EA treatment or allergic to acupuncture needles, 
pregnant or breastfeeding women; use of electrical stimulation devices 
(pacemaker or implantable defibrillator) severe infection or surgery 
around the local site of needling, chronic opioid use, conversion to open 
surgery, failure for paravertebral block (PVB). 
Randomization and blinding 
Patients were randomly assigned to either usual care(UC) or EA 
group by computer-generated codes and sequentially numbered, opaque 
envelopes. Given the characteristic of EA, the acupuncturist and patients 
were aware of the treatment allocation. Nevertheless, anesthetists, as­
sessors, the data collector as well as statisticians were blinded to treat­
ment allocation throughout the entire study. 
Anesthetic proceedings 
All surgeries were performed by senior surgeons using minimally 
invasive techniques by VATS. Anesthetic and surgical techniques were 
standardized. Anesthesia was induced with propofol 2 mg/kg and 
sufentanil 0.4 μg/kg. Tracheal intubation was facilitated with 0.1 mg/kg 
vecuronium. Anesthesia was maintained with target-controlled in­
fusions of propofol and remifentanil according to the hemodynamics 
and bispectral index. Ondansetron 4 mg and dexamethasone 5 mg were 
given before skin closure for prevention of PONV. Intravenous parecoxib 
40 mg was used as adjunctive pain management at the end of surgery. 
The endotracheal tube was removed after adequate spontaneous venti­
lation and movement resumed. 
Under ultrasound guidance, paravertebral nerve block with 20 ml of 
0.375 % ropivacaine were used for all patients. Both groups received 
routine oral celecoxib 200 mg every 12 h and the same postoperative 
intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). The PCA consisted of 
morphine 1 mg/kg, ketamine 1 mg/kg and tropisetron 10 mg, set at a 
basal rate of 2 ml/h, with a bolus dose of 0.5 ml and a lockout time of 15 
min. When patients reported a pain VAS score ≥4 or they requested an 
analgesic, additional bucinnazine 100 mg will be given intramuscularly. 
Electroacupuncture(EA) interventions 
EA was performed on patients laying supine by our team composed 
of experienced acupuncturists with at least 10 years of practice. The 
selection of acupoints were bilateral PC6 (Neiguan), ST36 (Zusanli), and 
ST37 (Shangjuxu), based on expert consensus provided by qualified and 
experienced acupuncturists and published literature14–18. Specific acu­
points are associated with certain regional effects. The locations of these 
acupuncture points are described in The National Standards for Acupoint 
Location19. Patients in the EA group received acupuncture 24 h prior to 
surgery, 4 h and 24 h after surgery, and each session lasted 30 min. After 
disinfection, single-use, sterile acupuncture needles (size 0.30 × 40 mm, 
Suzhou Medical Supplies Factory Co., Ltd., China) were inserted 
perpendicular into skin with a depth of approximately 20 to 45 mm. The 
needles were manipulated via lifting and rotation until the patient felt a 
De-qi sensation, defined as a feeling of heaviness, swelling or numbness 
around the acupoint20. The stimulator device we used was Huatuo 
Electronic Acupuncture Treatment Instrument (EA stimulator, XS-998B, 
Nanjing Xiaosong Medical Instrument Research Institute, Nanjing, 
China). The ipsilateral PC6, ST36 and ST37 will be separately connected 
to one electrode set, and therefore yielding three electrode sets. The 
stimulation frequency was 15 Hz, with intensities adjusted to trigger 
slight vibration of the needle handles according to individual tolerance. 
The EA group also received usual care (UC). 
Usual care 
Usual care was the routine treatments after thoracic surgery, 
including routine nursing care, analgesics, dietary instructions and 
discharge planning21. Early ambulation was encouraged. Oral feeding 
was resumed as early as possible. Participants who experienced absence 
of flatus over last 72 h postoperatively were instructed to use laxatives. 
PONV was treated with intravenous ondansetron 4 mg as needed. 
No acupuncture treatment was performed in the UC group. 
Outcome measures 
Primary outcomes of this study were time to first flatus and first 
defecation. 
Times to first flatus and defecation measured in hours were recorded 
by the patients and then reported to our research assistant. 
Secondary outcomes included incidence and degree of abdominal 
distension (AD), use of the rescue medication, incidence of PON and 
POV, VAS pain scores, postoperative morphine use, time to ambulation 
and length of hospital stay. Abdominal distension was measured by a 5- 
point Likert-type scale ranging from “0 = I feel no abdominal distension 
at all” to “4 = I feel horrible abdominal distension and need it 
addressed” 8,16,21. Pain intensity was rated on a 0–10 visual analogue 
scale (VAS, 0= no pain, 10= worst pain). Incidence of PON and POV 
(defined as at least one episode of nausea or vomiting that occurred), 
pain at rest and on movement were recorded at 0–24 h, 24–48 h and 
48–72 h after surgery. Postoperative opioid use for the first 24 h and 72 
h after surgery was extracted from the PCA device records.

Statistical analysis 
A sample size calculation was performed based on our feasibility 
study4. In that study, the mean reduction of postoperative time to first 
flatus was 3.3 h, favoring the EA group. To achieve a power of 0.9 and 
two-sided α of 0.05, the sample size for this study was targeted as 73 in 
each group. Considering a possible dropout rate of 20 %, a sample size of 
184 patients in total was needed in the study. 
We conducted our analyses using the intention-to-treat principles 
regardless of whether patients had received the assigned intervention or 
completed all EA sessions. Statistical analyses were performed using 
SPSS22.0 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, United States). Continuous 
data were expressed as mean(standard deviation) and median (inter­
quartile range), and categorical variables were expressed as frequency 
(percentage). T tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare the 
group differences on continuous variables as appropriate. Fisher test and 
the χ2 test were used to analyze categorical variables as appropriate. All 
tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically 
significant. 
Results 
A total of 184 patients were screened in the study, of whom, 180 
were randomly assigned to EA group (n = 90) or UC group (n = 90). 
After randomization, 3 patients withdrew from the trial because they 
were declined to continue EA on postoperative day 2 (n = 1), didn’t 
receive PVB (n = 1) or converted to open surgery (n = 1). These three 
patients were retained in the final analysis. Finally, 90 patients per 
group were included in the intention-to-treat analyses (Fig.1). The 
baseline demographic data and clinical characteristics of the two groups 
were comparable and did not differ significantly (Table1). 
The time to first flatus was 2 h shorter in the EA group as compared 
with that of the UC group (P = 0.004). The time to first defecation was 
more than 4 h shorter, than UC group, P = 0.002)(Table 2). 
The incidence of abdominal distension was significantly reduced in 
the EA group (34.4% vs. 51.1 %, P = 0.024). There was a trend toward 
decreased degree of abdominal distension, but did not reach a signifi­
cant difference (P = 0.069). Patients who required rescue medication 
were also significantly less in the EA group than UC group (53.3% vs. 
71.1 %, P = 0.014) (Table 2). 
26.7 % participants in the EA group, compared with 44.4 % in the UC 
group experienced PON in the 0–24 h after surgery. Also, the incidence 
of 0–24 h POV(13.3 %) in Group EA was significantly lower than that in 
Group UC(27.8 %). There were no significant differences in the inci­
dence of PONV at 24–48 h. 
Post operative pain was minimal, about 2/10 in both groups. There 
were no significant differences in the post-operative pain at rest or on 
movement between groups. Cumulative morphine consumption during 
the postoperative day 1 and 3 were similar between the two groups.

Discussion 
This trial examined the effect of perioperative EA on gastrointestinal 
function after thoracoscopic lung surgery. Our results showed that 
perioperative EA led to a significantly shorter time to first flatus and first 
defecation, decreased incidence and a trend toward alleviated abdom­
inal distension and reduced incidence of PONV during first 24 h, 
reflecting a multi-facet effect of EA on the gastrointestinal functional 
recovery. 
Constipation and impaired defecation pattern following thoracic 
surgery contributes to delayed recovery3,4 and costs to the healthcare 
system22, and is often overlooked in patient care3. Systemic prokinetic 
drugs are often used for POI, but the evidence is insufficient to recom­
mend the use of cholecystokinin-like drugs, cisapride, dopamine an­
tagonists, propranolol or vasopressin23. Effective non-pharmacological 
effort that can reduce patient suffering and improve recovery should 
be properly tested and encouraged. Our study showed that EA, in 
addition to UC, was associated with shorter time to first flatus and first 
defecation. Also, EA group showed decreased incidence and degree of 
abdominal distension and rescue medication. This result was in consis­
tent with previous randomized trials and meta-analyses demonstrating 
positive effects of acupuncture in abdominal surgery14–17. Our results 
suggested that EA offered additional clinical benefit to patients under­
going thoracoscopic lung surgery, but placebo effects cannot be 
excluded without a sham acupuncture control 24. 
The selection of acupoints for our study were PC6, ST36, and ST37, 
which was based on evidence from published studies and the theories of 
Chinese medicine19,25,26. EA at PC6 not only has antiemetic effects, but 
also increases gastric motility via promoting efferent vagus nerve ac­
tivity27,28. ST36 and ST37 are located along the “Stomach Meridian” of 
Foot-Yangming on the anterior-tibia region. ST36 is at the junction of the 
Stomach Meridian and plays an important role in balancing Yin and 
Yang in the abdomen to relieve distention and constipation. Stimulation 
at ST36 can enhance the small intestinal motility, possibly by 
down-regulating the concentration of vasoactive intestinal peptide and 
up-regulating the concentrations of motilin, ghrelin and gastrin29. ST37 
is an acupoint that mainly regulates colon motility to assist gas passage. 
EA stimulation at ST37 increases defaecation frequency and gastroin­
testinal transit markers of constipation, such as serotonin and trypto­
phan hydroxylase concentrations26. In general, EA improved the 
gastrointestinal tract transit function by activating the vagal nerve 
directly and regulating peripheral inflammation27,28,30. A combination 
of these three points are the preferred acupoints for treating POI, 
contributing to the encouraging results of our study. 
The optimal timing and duration of EA in thoracic surgery has not 
been studied before. The majority of studies used EA postoperatively 
until the first flatus or defecation14–17,31. In one study conducted by Li S 
et al., one- session EA 24 h prior to the surgery also accelerated motility 
of the gastrointestinal track32. Liu MY et al. 21. found that, when 
compared with no acupuncture treatment, EA 24 h before surgery 
reduced the incidence and degree of abdominal distension and short­
ened the time to first defecation and duration of hospitalization after 
vascular laparotomy. In our study, EA stimulation was applied both pre- 
and post-operatively. Consistent with our feasibility study4, most pa­
tients were satisfied with the frequency and duration of acupuncture, 
and only one patient discontinued EA postoperatively due to discomfort. 
Our protocol of three treatment sessions may seem to demand patients’ 
time, it was well accepted4. From clinical point of view, it might be 
easier to use single session before or after surgery. Future research is 
needed to identify optimal timing and regimen of EA to enhance 
gastrointestinal recovery, to assess potential patients’ satisfaction and 
cost-effectiveness with this intervention. 
Studies have used different endpoints to record POI. It is important to 
define sensitive and objective measures that accurately captures the 
spectrum of POI. Time to first flatus and defecation are easy and simple 
components in the assessment of gastrointestinal dysfunction and 
POI21,25,31,33. However, available evidence for these indicators is 
limited. For example, time to first flatus is difficult to assess accurately 
and time to first defecation might simply reflect rectal emptying and 
provide no reliable information on the recovery of whole gut. Bowel 
movements are the most reliable endpoint, real-time bowel sound 
recognition using wearable health monitoring should be used in future 
trials. In our institution, early oral feeding as part of ERAS is started 
without the return of bowel sounds after thoracic surgery, so we didn’t 
monitor intestinal sounds. 
PONV is common in surgical patients and represents a frequent and 
critical cause of delayed recovery. Acupuncture on P6 has been widely 
used to reduce PONV after several types of surgery11,13. Despite PONV 
prophylaxis with dexamethasone and ondansetron, PONV incidence was 
still high in both groups. The 44.4 % and 27.8 % incidence of respective 
PON and POV in the UC group were a bit higher, compared with 16.9 % 
reported by the literature on this form of surgery34. Individual suscep­
tibility and opioid use such as morphine PCA are strongly regarded as 
the most likely causes of PONV in this study. It is pleasing to see in our 
study, EA significantly reduced the incidence of nausea and vomiting on 
the 1st postoperative day. Combination with EA and routine antiemetics 
may offer additional benefit for those patients.

required to restore normal gastrointestinal function35. Sun Y, et al. 
found that acupuncture treatment reduced opioid consumption, pain 
intensity and opioid-related side-effects36. On the contrary, we didn’t

find any beneficial effect of EA on pain scores and opioid consumption 
after surgery. A few reasons might explain this: 1) our study only focus 
on thoracoscopic lung surgery, which is a less painful surgery, when 
compared with open abdominal surgery. In Sun’s review, all forms of 
surgeries were included; 2) we used multimodal analgesia to relieve pain 
and minimize opioid use. Multimodal analgesia was achieved with 
nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, PVB and morphine PCA, pro­
ducing sufficient analgesia after VATS with pain score less than 4/1037. 
In addition, the use of PVB might have underestimated the analgesic 
effects of EA by blocking both somatic and sympathetic neural path­
ways, which might be considered as a confounding factor on the effects 
of EA38. 
Postoperative gastrointestinal recovery is an crucial part in the 
enhanced recovery of surgical patients. Ng SS, et al. demonstrated that 
EA was associated with shorter hospital stay after surgery, when a fast- 
track perioperative program was not available in their hospital16. Liu T, 
et al. 39. reported that ambulation within 2 h of extubation was safe and 
feasible and could lead to shorter length of hospital stay and time to first 
flatus in patients with undergoing thoracoscopic lung surgery. In our 
study, both groups had similar time to ambulation and hospital stay, 
because we applied enhanced recovery program during our routine 
clinical practice. 
There were certain limitations to this study. First, a placebo effect 
can’t be excluded without a sham EA group. Because sham EA was not 
used in this study, the inadvertent interaction between assessors and 
patient in the UC group may affect the results. However, according to a 
Cochrane review, inclusion of a sham EA group would not have changed 
the research conclusions40. Second, lack of standardized outcome mea­
sures may limit the interpretation of the findings. Finally, this study only 
investigated the effect of EA on bowel motility, PONV and postoperative 
pain in a short period. The incidence of postoperative constipation and 
chronic pain may last for a long time after surgery3,41, so the long term 
effects of EA on gastrointestinal function and chronic pain in patients 
undergoing thoracic surgery needs further study. 
Conclusion 
Despite certain limitations, we provide evidence that perioperative 
EA, in combination with standard usual care, has multi-facet effect and 
can lead to a faster recovery of gastrointestinal function following 
thoracoscopic surgery.
