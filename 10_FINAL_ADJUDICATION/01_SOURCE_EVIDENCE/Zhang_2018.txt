Introduction
The postoperative disturbance is a frequent complication after 
abdominal surgery which is clinically characterized by delayed 
passage of gas and stool, resumption of oral feeding, postopera-
tive ambulation and postoperative pain, and results in prolonged 
postoperative hospital stay and increased healthcare costs [1–3]. 
According to the Health Care Financing Administration (HCFA) 
data, Medicare patients who underwent surgical procedures 
stayed in the hospital for a total of 1.82 million days (a mean of 
11.3 days per patient), and cost healthcare industries approxi-
mately US $1.75 billion during the year of 2000 [4]. It is estimated 
that the annual healthcare system costs will be reduced by 155 
million dollars if the hospital stay for each patient is reduced by 1 
day with the major abdominal colorectal surgeries in the United 
States [4].
Pharmacological treatments available for patients after major 
abdominal surgeries have showed limited effects [5]. Investigations

A Pilot Study Evaluating Improvement in Post-Operative 
Recovery
Bo Zhang, MD1,2, Feng Xu, MD3, Pingping Hu, MD3, Mingyuan Zhang, MD4, Kehui Tong, MD4, Gang Ma, MD2,5, Yuemei Xu, MD3, 
Liang Zhu, MD1 and Jiande D. Z. Chen, PhD2,6
Background: Functional gastrointestinal disturbance occurs after abdominal surgeries and could last for an 
extended period of time in some cases. This study was designed (1) to evaluate the effects of 
needleless transcutaneous electrical acustimulation (TEA) on postoperative recovery, and (2) to 
investigate the mechanisms involving autonomic function in postoperative patients after removal of 
gastrointestinal cancers.
Methods: 
Forty-two patients (33 male, age: 69.5 ± 1.5 years) scheduled for abdominal surgical removal 
of gastrointestinal cancers were randomized to TEA (n = 21) and sham-TEA (n = 21). TEA was 
performed via acupoints ST36 and PC6 1 h twice daily from the postoperative day (POD) 1 to day 3. 
Sham-TEA was performed at non-acupoints.
Results: 
(1) TEA improved major postoperative symptoms by about 30%, including a reduction in time to 
defecation by 31.7% (P < 0.01 vs. sham-TEA), time to first flatus by 35.9% (P < 0.001), time to 
ambulation by 42.8% (P < 0.01), time to resuming diet by 26.5% (P < 0.01) and hospital stay 
by 30% (P < 0.05) as well as pain score by 50% (P < 0.01). (2) TEA significantly increased vagal 
activity (P < 0.001) and decreased sympathetic activity on POD 4 (P < 0.001) compared with POD 
1 as well as the serum level of NE (P < 0.05). (3) The vagal activity, high frequency assessed from 
the spectral analysis of heart rate variability, was negatively correlated with time to resuming diet, 
whereas the sympathetic measurement, serum norepinephrine was positively correlated with time to 
resuming diet and time to flatus. (4) TEA but not sham-TEA decreased TNF-α by 17.4% from POD 1 
to POD 4. (5) TEA was an independent predictor of a shorter hospital stay.
Conclusions: Needleless TEA improves major postoperative symptoms by enhancing vagal and suppressing 
sympathetic activities.

ileus (POI) have come up with a negative conclusion. In 2008, a 
Cochrane review included 39 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) 
and investigated 15 systematic prokinetic drugs but could not 
identify any beneficial effects on time of first defecation and time 
of resuming diet after surgeries [6]. In the review, Alvimopan was 
shown to have a beneficial effect in several studies but the authors 
found there were many methodological biases. In a recent review, 
prucalopride was reported to shorten duration of POI and reduce 
the inflammatory response [7], but multi-center RCTs are needed 
to investigate which kind of patient would gain the most from this 
medicine [8].
It was well known that autonomic neurological dysfunction 
plays an important role in the progression of impaired gastro-
intestinal motility [9]. Early studies [10, 11] have shown that 
abdominal surgery increases sympathetic activity and decreases 
vagal activity. During the surgery, afferent signals are transmit-
ted to the brainstem where they trigger an increased autonomic 
output to the neurons of the intermediolateral column of the 
thoracic cord, where sympathetic preganglionic neurons (releas-
ing noradrenaline (NA)) are located [12–14]. In addition to this 
sympathetic overactivity, intestinal manipulation also activates 
resident macrophages in the intestinal muscularis externa which 
release proinflammatory cytokines and result in a long-lasting 
phase of POI [15].
Acupuncture is a traditional Chinese medicine that has been 
practiced for several thousands of years in China [16]. It has 
been used to treat functional disorders, including chemotherapy-
induced nausea [17], postoperative nausea and vomiting [18, 19], 
irritable bowel syndrome, diarrhea [20] and constipation [21]. 
Electroacupuncture (EA) uses electrical pulses instead of manual 
manipulations to stimulate acupoints and appears to show more 
consistent and reproducible results [16]. In human studies, EA 
at ST36 has been shown to accelerate postoperative gastrointes-
tinal function recovery [22]. In animal studies, EA is generally 
considered to enhance gastrointestinal motility by activating the 
vagus nerve and inhibiting sympathetic activity [23–26]. However, 
acupuncture and EA both use needles and this limits widespread 
applications since they have to be administrated by healthcare pro-
fessionals.
Transcutaneous electrical acustimulation (TEA) is a newly 
developed method of EA by replacing needles with surface elec-
trodes. It has been shown to improve chemotherapy-induced 
gastric symptoms, cold meal-induced impaired gastric accommo-
dation [27, 28] and chronic constipation [29]. TEA has been con-
sistently shown to enhance gastrointestinal motility by improving 
vagal activity [9, 30]. However, it is unknown whether TEA might 
have a therapeutic effect on postoperative recovery. We hypoth-
esized that major abdominal surgeries activate sympathetic nerve 
thus impair gastrointestinal (GI) motility and that TEA antago-
nizes surgery-induced sympathetic overactivity and therefore 
improves postoperative GI motility.
The aims of our study were to investigate the effects of TEA on 
postoperative recovery and to study the mechanisms involving 
autonomic function in patients with gastrointestinal cancers after 
major abdominal surgeries.
Methods
Study participants
The study was performed from June 2016 through December 
2016 at the Yinzhou People’s Hospital. Forty-two patients with 
gastrointestinal cancers after open abdominal surgeries aged 
over 18 years were enrolled in this study. The sample size was 
estimated using previous data and power analysis. Forty subjects 
were required to detect a difference of at least 15% in time to first 
defecation after surgery between the TEA and sham-TEA groups 
with a conventional power of 80% and an α value of 0.05. The 
study protocol was approved by the ethical committee of Yinzhou 
People’s Hospital. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) >18 years 
of age, (2) underwent an open abdominal surgery for cancers and 
(3) willing to sign a written informed consent. Exclusion criteria 
were: (1) diabetes, (2) multiple surgeries or single surgery with 
complications, (3) allergic to skin preparation and (4) familiar 
with acupoints and their functions.
Study design
Patients were recruited in the study immediately after surgery if all 
inclusion and exclusion criteria were fulfilled. Patients were rand-
omized into two groups: TEA and sham-TEA. The postoperative 
management was the same between the two groups. Demographic 
data and surgical details were recorded. A blood sample was taken 
at 7:00 a.m. and placed on ice, and an electrocardiogram (ECG) 
was made from 8:30 a.m. to 9:00 a.m. on postoperative day (POD) 
1 and POD 4. Each patient underwent two sessions of TEA or 
sham-TEA daily from POD 1 to POD 3. The visual analog scale 
(VAS) pain score, time of the first defecation, time of the first flat-
ulence, time to resume diet and the length of the postoperative 
hospital stay were noted.
TEA and sham-TEA
Acupoints ST36 and PC6 were used for TEA [29–31]. ST36 is 
located at the place of four-finger-breadth measuring down from 
the outer eye of the knee between the fibula and the tibia, one-
finger-breadth measurement beside the tibia. PC6 is located at the 
place of one-sixth of remote end and five-sixths of the proximal 
end of the connection stripe between the transverse wrist crease 
and cubical crease. A pair of electrodes were placed at bilateral 
ST36 and another pair of electrodes were placed at bilateral PC6 
(Fig. 1). Each pair of electrodes were connected to a watch-size 
digital stimulator (SNM-FDC01, Ningbo Maida Medical Device 
Inc., Ningbo, China). The stimulation parameters for both stimu-
lators were set at 2 s on and 3 s off, pulse width of 0.5 ms, pulse 
frequency of 25 Hz and amplitude of 2–10 mA (at the maximum 
level tolerated by the subject) based on previous studies [29–31]. 
The TEA treatment was given for 1 h, twice daily (9–10 a.m. and 
4–5 p.m.), lasting for 3 days.
Sham-TEA was the same except that non-acupoints were used 
to replace ST36 and PC6. The sham-acupoint for PC6 was located 
at about 15–20 cm away from PC6 (up to the elbow and outside of 
the coastal margin of the forearm not on any meridian) and the 
sham-point for ST36 was located at 10–15 cm down from and to 
the lateral side of ST36 not on any meridian [29–31].

Outcome measurements
Main outcome measurements included time to the first defecation 
[22] and time to resume diet [32] after surgery. Other measure-
ments included time to ambulation, length of postoperative hos-
pital stay and the VAS pain score on the first 3 postoperative days. 
The VAS digital score was from 0 to 10; 0: painless; 1–3: mild and 
endurable pain; 4–6: moderate pain affecting sleep but still endur-
able; 7–10: severe pain, unbearable, affecting appetite and sleep. It 
was recorded by a caring physician who did not know the study 
design.
Assessment of autonomic function
The autonomic function was assessed by the specatral analysis of 
heart rate variability (HRV) derived from the ECG, described as 
follows: three locations in the chest/abdomen where ECG elec-
trodes were to be placed (apical, the second intercostal of sternal 
right edge, and the intersection of right rib arch and clavicle mid-
line) were marked, gently rubbed with sandy gel and wiped clean 
with a gauze [33]. Electrodes were placed over the treated skin, 
and the patient was asked to lie down and stay quiet. The ECG 
was recorded using a special amplifier with a recording frequency 
of 100 Hz (ECG-201, Ningbo Maida Medical Device Inc., Ningbo, 
China). The HRV signal was obtained by identifying the R peak 
and calculating the RR interval from the ECG using a custom-
made software. The HRV signal was subjected to power spec-
trum analysis and the power in different bands was calculated. 
The power in the low-frequency (LF) band (0.04–0.15 Hz) reflects 
mainly sympathetic activity, whereas the power in the high-fre-
quency (HF) band (0.15–0.50 Hz) represents purely parasym-
pathetic or vagal activity. In this study, normalized values were 
utilized: that is, LF was calculated as LF/(HF + LF) and HF was 
calculated as HF/(HF + LF) [33, 34].
Blood draw and assay
A blood sample was taken at 7 a.m. on POD 1 and POD 4. About 
5 ml of blood was drawn into a procoagulant tube placed on ice 
and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 8 a.m. (1 h later). The 
serum was divided into 3 portions, each about 0.5 ml and placed at 
−80 °C for assay within 6 months. Human enzyme-linked immu-
nosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Roche Company, American, Cat. 
No. 0734-2014, Shanghai Shiyan Biological Technology Co., Ltd, 
China, Lot. No. 201704) were used for the analysis of interleu-
kin-6 (IL-6) and noradrenaline (NE), respectively. Tumor necro-
sis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) were analyzed 
by human ELISA kits (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Products 
Limited Co., UK, Cat. No. LKNF1 and Cat. No. LKL11, respec-
tively). All blood assays were performed blindly by a professional 
company (Shanghai Dian Medical Testing Laboratory).
Statistical analysis
The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the distribution of major 
data, including time to defecation, flatus and diet, and the dis-
tributions were found normal and therefore parametric analyses 
were used. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). 
P < 0.05 was considered statistical significance. Student’s t-test 
was performed to assess the difference in the measurement (time 
to defecation, time to flatulence, time to resume diet, length of 
postoperative hospital stay or VAS pain score) between sham-TEA 
and TEA group. Paired Student’s t-test was used to compare the 
difference in serum NE, TNF-α, and IL-6, and HF (vagal activity) 
and LF/HF (sympathovagal ratio) on POD 1 and POD 4 in each 
group. Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to assess the 
correlation between the endpoint measures and other parameters. 
Multivariate analysis with multiple linear regression was used to 
identify independent predictors of outcome measures.
Time to defecation higher than 96 h was defined as the end-
point event. Comparison between endpoint and non-endpoint 
groups was performed using Chi-square Analysis, and P values of 
<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Clinic pathological 
parameters (P < 0.15) were tested by stepwise multivariate logistic 
regression analysis, and P values of <0.05 were considered statis-
tically significant. The HF on POD 4 in patients with suspected 
delayed recovery was assessed and evaluated by receiver operat-
ing characteristic (ROC) curve. Data were analyzed by statistical 
software SPSS 19.0.
Results
Characteristics of patients
A total of 42 patients were recruited and randomized into sham-
TEA (21 patients) and TEA (21 patients) groups. Demographic 
data and surgical details are shown in Table  1. There were no 
significant differences in gender, age or body mass index (BMI) 
between the two groups (all P > 0.05). The two groups were simi-
lar in terms of surgical details and no significant difference was 
noted in the number of patients who underwent any particular 
type of surgery between the TEA and sham-TEA groups.

TEA improved postoperative recovery
TEA shortened the recovery time of clinic symptoms. Com-
pared to sham-TEA, TEA shortened time to defecation by 
31.7% (P < 0.01) and time to first flatus by 35.9% (P < 0.001) 
(Fig. 2a). Prolonged postoperative ileus as defined in a previ-
ous study [35] was noted in 5 patients in the Sham-TEA group 
but none in the TEA group (Chi-square analysis, P < 0.05). 
TEA reduced time to ambulation by 42.8% (P < 0.01), time to 
resuming diet by 26.5% (P < 0.01) and length of postoperative 
hospital stay by 30.2% (P < 0.05) in comparison of sham-TEA 
(Fig. 2b).
TEA reduced the VAS pain score on the postoperative Day 2 by 
50.8% (P < 0.01) and Day 3 by 64.9% (P < 0.001) in comparison 
with sham-TEA (Fig. 2c).
Effects of surgeries and TEA on inflammatory cytokines
TEA but not sham-TEA decreased the serum level of TNF-α by 
17.4% from POD 1 to POD 4 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3a). The TNF-α on 
POD 4 was positively correlated with VAS pain scores on POD 2 
(r = 0.410, P < 0.01) and POD 3 (r = 0.402, P < 0.05).
No significant difference was noted in the serum level of IL-6 
between the TEA and sham-TEA groups (Fig. 3b); nor was there a 
difference between POD 1 and POD 4. The level of IL-6 on POD 1 
was actually comparable to that in healthy subjects [36], suggesting 
that the surgical procedure did not alter IL-6.
The serum level of IL-1β was too low to detect in any of the sam-
ples, suggesting that it was not elevated by surgery.
Mechanisms of TEA involving autonomic functions
TEA enhanced vagal activity. Vagal activity (HF) was increased 
from POD1 to POD 4 with TEA (P < 0.001) but not with sham-
TEA (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4a). Similarly, the sympathovagal ratio (LF/
HF) was decreased significantly with TEA (P < 0.001), but not 
with sham-TEA (P > 0.05) (Fig.  4b). TEA but not sham-TEA 
decreased the serum level of NE by 42.5% from POD 1 to POD 4 
(P < 0.05) (Fig. 4c).
The HF on POD 1 and 4 were negatively correlated with NE 
on POD 1 (r = −0.353, P < 0.05) and NE on POD 4 (r = −0.328, 
P < 0.05), respectively, as well as on both of two days (r = −0.353, 
P < 0.01), suggesting an agreement between these two measures of 
the autonomic function.
In addition to the direct effect of TEA on the vagal activity 
assessed by the HF, the HF was also found to be associated with a 
number of clinical outcome measures and even capable of predict-
ing certain clinical outcomes as shown below:

Discussion
In this study, we found that: (1) TEA improved major postopera-
tive symptoms, including a reduction in time to first defecation, 
time to first flatus, time to ambulation, time to resuming diet 
and hospital stay as well as pain score. (2) TEA but not sham-
TEA decreased the serum level of TNF-α. (3) TEA significantly 
increased vagal activity and decreased sympathetic activity as well 
as the serum level of NE. (4) The vagal activity, HF, assessed from 
the spectral analysis of HRV was negatively correlated with time 
to resuming diet, whereas the sympathetic measurement, serum 
norepinephrine was positively correlated with time to resuming 
diet and time to flatus. (5) TEA was an independent predictor for 
a shorter hospital stay.
While the ameliorating effect of needle-based EA on postop-
erative recovery had been reported [22] it was unclear whether 
needleless TEA would have a similar therapeutic effect on post-
operative recovery. We designed a prospective randomized sham-
controlled study to investigate the effects of TEA for postoperative 
recovery and its mechanisms involving autonomic functions. The 
newly developed method of TEA, which is needleless and can be

and medications. In this study, there was no significant difference 
in gender, age, BMI, severity of disease or surgical procedures 
between the two groups. In order to avoid placebo effects, the 
sham-controlled group was treated with the same electrical stimu-
lation at the non-acupoints [9, 28, 30].
Substantial reductions were noted in major postoperative symp-
toms with the noninvasive treatment of TEA. TEA shortened time 
to defecation and time to flatulence by more than 30%, and time 
to resume diet by 26.5% in comparison of sham-TEA. In addition, 
TEA reduced length of postoperative hospital stay by 30.2% and 
was found to be an independent predictor of a shorter hospital 
stay. In a previously clinic study, needle-based EA was reported 
to reduce time to defecation by 29.6% and the hospital stay by 
23.5% compared with control in patients after laparoscopic sur-
gery for colorectal cancer [22]. They also found that EA was an 
independent predictor of shorter duration of postoperative recov-
ery. In comparison with the previous EA therapy, the TEA method 
used in this study has an obvious advantage of being needleless: 
the therapy can be administrated by anyone including the patient. 
To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study to demon-
strate the therapeutic effects of needleless TEA for postoperative 
recovery.
The best indication of GI motility recovery is the combined 
measure tolerance of solid food and passage of stool [32]. Accord-
ingly, we speculated that these improvements were probably 
attributed to improvement in GI motility. GI motility was not 
assessed in this study due to invasiveness of the procedure and 
severity of the disease and surgical procedures in these patients. 
However, previous EA and TEA studies have suggested a possi-
ble role of improved gastric, intestinal and colonic motility. EA at 
ST36 significantly accelerated gastric emptying of solids in nor-
mal rats [37] and in a rodent model of delayed gastric emptying 
induced by restraint stress [38]. Ouyang et al. [39] reported that 
EA at PC6 and ST36 significantly improved gastric emptying of 
liquid in dogs with delayed gastric emptying induced by duodenal 
distention. EA at PC6 and ST36 improved gastric emptying of sol-
ids measured by scintigraphy in patients with gastroparesis [40]. 
Improvement of intestinal motility with EA has been reported in 
animals [38, 41, 42]. Enhancement of colonic motility with EA 
or TEA has been investigated in both animals and humans. In 
conscious rats, contractility of the distal colon and colonic transit 
were significantly increased by EA [43]. Recent studies reported 
that EA at ST36 improved colonic contractions and transit 
impaired by rectal distention [44] and surgery [23] by vagal activ-
ity enhancement. Similar findings were also investigated in TEA 
studies. Zhang et al. [30] found that the TEA treatment at ST36 
could improve the stress-induced gastric slow wave impaired by 
balancing sympathovagal activities. Huang et al. [28] found that 
TEA at ST36 and PC6 improved gastric accommodation, gastric 
slow wave damage and dyspeptic symptoms induced by cool meal 
in healthy volunteers. TEA has been shown to improve chronic 
constipation [29]. These previous consistent and positive findings 
support a prokinetic mechanism of TEA involved in the present 
clinical study.
Another noticeable finding of this study was that TEA reduced 
the VAS pain score by more than 50% on POD 2 and POD 3 in 
comparison of sham-TEA. Previous studies have suggested that EA 
blocks pain by activating a variety of bioactive chemicals through 
peripheral, spinal and supraspinal mechanisms. These include opi-
oids which reduce proinflammatory cytokines and norepinephrine 
[45]. The analgesic effect of TEA observed in the current study was 
also believed to be attributed to these previously reported opioid 
and sympathetic mechanisms.
The effects of the surgery on postoperative gastrointestinal dis-
turbance observed in this study might be secondary to changes in 
autonomic functions. It was well known that the autonomic neu-
rological dysfunction plays an important role in the progression of 
impaired gastrointestinal motility [9]. Early studies [10, 11] have 
shown that surgery increases sympathetic activity and decreases 
vagal activity. In the present study, using the spectral analysis of 
the HRV, a noninvasive and simple method which has been well 
documented to be an accurate and reliable method for the quan-
titative evaluation of parasympathetic activity and sympathova-
gal balance [46, 47], we found that TEA increased vagal activity 
(HF) and restored the sympathovagal balance (LF/HF). Similar 
autonomic mechanisms were also reported in other EA and TEA 
studies. EA at ST36 and PC6 with appropriate parameters has 
been consistently shown to activate the vagus nerve and decrease 
sympathovagal balance [23–26, 44]. The needleless TEA has also 
been reported to be able to balance the sympathovagal activities 
under various conditions of sympathetic overactivity [9, 30, 48]. 
In the present study, in addition to the assessment of autonomic 
functions via the spectral analysis of HRV that provided accurate 
measures of parasympathetic/vagal activity and the sympathovagal 
ratio, we also measured the serum level of NE that is an indicator of 
sympathetic neural activity [49, 50]. Sympathetic neural reflex has 
been proposed as one of the predominant mechanism of POI [51, 
52]. Fukuda et al. [53] reported that sympathetic-adrenergic path-
way mediated entire GI tract motility at the early and late stages 
of postoperative disturbance and this pathway could be inhibited 
by α-2-adrenoceptor agonist. While the exact neural pathway was 
not investigated in the current study, previous animal studies [23, 
54] have reported that: (1) EA at ST36 activates nucleus tractus 
solitaries by activating somatic afferent nerves, demonstrating the 
involvement of the central nervous system; (2) the afferent signal 
simulates the dorsal motor nucleus of vagus, resulting in increased 
vagal efferent flow to visceral organs; and (3) the afferent signal is 
conveyed to other parts of the central nervous system, resulting in 
a reduced sympathetic outflow. Intuitively, the effects of TEA on 
postoperative recovery might follow the same pathways as EA: TEA 
activated somatic nerves that conveyed a signal to the brain via the 
spinal afferent and the brain processed the signal and produced an 
enhanced vagal efferent to the GI tract, resulting in improvement 
in motility and reduction in postoperative symptoms.

dominant mechanism of POI [51, 52]. In the current study, TEA 
decreased serum TNF-α on POD 4 in comparison of POD 1. In a 
previous animal study, needle-based EA decreased the plasma con-
centration of TNF-α in a rodent model of 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene

[55]. In a clinical study, EA decreased the serum level of TNF-α 
in patients with rheumatoid arthritis [56]. De Jonge et al. [51] 
reported that intestinal manipulation during major surgery 
increased inflammatory infiltrates and then triggered the inhibi-
tory enterogastric neurons pathway which resulted in POI in 
mice. Later, De Jonge at al. [57] found that vagal activity attenu-

the secretion of TNF-α via alpha7 subunit-mediated Jak2-STAT3 
(Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) 
activation in intestinal macrophages in a mouse model of intes-

that vagal activity reduced intestinal inflammation and amelio-
rated POI through α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor activity in 
the intestine muscularis-resident macrophages in a mouse model 
of intestinal manipulation. Accordingly, we speculate that TEA 
decreased TNF-α by activating cholinergic anti-inflammatory 
pathway due to its enhancement in vagal activity. Furthermore, a 
number of recent studies have shown the role of inflammation in 
animal models of POI and suggested that improvement in inflam-
mation may lead to restoration of gastrointestinal motility. Farro et 
al. [59] reported a critical role of monocyte-derived macrophages 
in restoring intestinal hemostasis after surgical trauma. Deng et 
al. [60] reported an ameliorating effect of acupuncture on POI by 
suppressing inflammation and protecting interstitial cells of Cajal, 
resulting in improved intestinal transit.
Surprisingly, the current study failed to show an increase of 
serum IL-6 or IL-1β. The level of IL-6 measured in this study was 
comparable to that in healthy controls [36], suggesting that IL-6 
was not sufficiently elevated due to the surgeries. Similarly, the 
level of IL-1β was found undetectable as reported in healthy con-
trols [61].
Other interesting results of this study were the correlations of 
the postoperative symptoms and the autonomic functions. The 
vagal activity (HF) was negatively correlated with time to resume 
diet and serum NE was positively correlated with time to resume 
diet and time to flatus. These were interesting correlation findings 
that had never been reported previously suggested again the pos-
sible mechanisms involved in the improvement of postoperative 
symptoms with TEA: TEA improved GI motility by suppressing 
sympathetic activity and enhancing vagal activity, resulting in 
improvement in postoperative symptoms. Also, we found a nega-
tive correlation between the vagal activity assessed from the spec-
tral analysis of HRV and the serum level of NE, demonstrating that 
these two measurements were consistent and complementary with 
each other [62, 63].
Furthermore, we discovered that HF <0.45 was an independ-
ent risk factor for predicting the occurrence of longer postopera-
tive recovery or postoperative ileus. Our findings indicated that if 
the vagal activity, HF assessed from the spectral analysis of HRV, 
was lower than 0.45 on POD 4, the patient would need more time 
to recover from the surgery. This suggests that in postoperative 
patients, the HF should be monitored constantly and more aggres-
sive management (prokinetic therapy) should be considered if this 
value is low.
There were several limitations in our study. First, patients had a 
long duration of hospital stay in comparison with that in western 
countries. This was attributed to the social background, such as 
culture and tradition, and hospitalization costs. In China, due to 
relatively low costs of hospital stay and serious concerns of both 
patients and surgeons on major invasive surgical procedures and 
postsurgical recovery, patients stay at hospital much longer to 
ensure a complete recovery. Second, symptoms of upper abdom-
inal fullness, nausea and vomiting after the surgery were not 
recorded. However, due to the fact that very few patients had these 
symptoms, it was unlikely to observe a significant difference in 
these symptoms between the sham-TEA and TEA groups. Third, 
this was a single center study and some bias might be present in 
the study.
In conclusion, needleless TEA at PC6 and ST36 is able to accel-
erate postoperative recovery after open abdominal surgeries in 
patients with GI cancers by enhancing vagal activity. Needleless 
and self-administrable TEA may be considered as an easy-to-
implement and low-cost complementary therapy to improve post-
operative recovery.
