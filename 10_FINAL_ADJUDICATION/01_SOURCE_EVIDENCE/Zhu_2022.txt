Preoperative electroacupuncture for 
postoperative nausea and vomiting in 
laparoscopic gynecological surgery: a 
randomized controlled trial
Juan Zhu1*, Sha Li1*, Wenzhong Wu2, Jie Guo1, Xiaoqiu Wang2, 
Guang Yang1, Zhigang Lu3, Fangbing Ji1, Rong Zou1, 
Zhen Zheng4
 and Man Zheng1
Abstract
Objective: We aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of preoperative electroacupuncture (EA) on the inci-
dence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and severity of postoperative pain, in gynecological patients 
undergoing laparoscopic surgery. The effects of EA administered at different preoperative time points were compared.
Methods: A total of 413 patients undergoing elective laparoscopic gynecological surgery were randomly allocated into 
4 groups receiving EA the day before surgery (Group Pre, n = 103), 30 min before (Group 30, n = 104) or both (Group 
Comb, n = 103), or usual care alone (Group Usual, n = 103). All acupuncture groups had usual care. The incidence of 
PONV and pain at 24 h were primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included the severity of postoperative nausea, 
vomiting and pain, requirement for antiemetic medication and quality of recovery (QoR)-15 scores after surgery.
Results: There were significant differences between the four groups in nausea and vomiting incidence (0–24 h), postop-
erative antiemetic use (0–48 h), and postoperative pain (0–6 h), with the EA groups recording the lowest levels. Regard-
ing primary outcomes, incidence of nausea and vomiting at 6-24 h was 28/11/18/11% (p = 0.003) 23/5/8/9% (p < 0.001), 
respectively, for Groups Usual/Pre/30/Comb. Accordingly, EA reduced the incidence of nausea and vomiting at 6-24 h by 
61/34/60% and 79/65/61% for Groups Pre/30/Comb, respectively. Regarding secondary outcomes, incidence of nausea and 
vomiting at 0-6 h was 20/9/11/7% (p = 0.013) and 17/7/9/6% (p = 0.021), respectively, for Groups Usual/Pre/30/Comb. Res-
cue antiemetics at 0–6 h were required by 18/4/11/4% (p = 0.001) in Groups Usual/Pre/30/Comb. The mean numerical rating 
scale (NRS) pain score (0–10) at 0–6 h was significantly different between groups (2.45/1.89/2.01/1.97 for Groups Usual/
Pre/30/Comb, p = 0.024). There were no significant differences between the three EA-treated groups.
Conclusion: In gynecological patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery and treated with multimodal antiemetic meth-
ods, one session of preoperative EA may be a safe adjunctive treatment for PONV prophylaxis. Optimal timing of EA 
requires further verification.

Introduction
Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is a frequent 
and unpleasant complaint after anesthesia, affecting 20%–
30% of patients,1 and up to 80% of high-risk patients.2 
PONV and postoperative pain are undesirable for both 
patients and clinicians,3 and are the leading causes of 
unplanned admission following laparoscopic gynecological 
day surgery, leading to higher costs.4 Female sex of patients, 
laparoscopic surgery, gynecological procedures and opioid 
analgesia are all independent risk factors for PONV.5,6
Drug therapies cannot fully relieve PONV and may 
cause side effects such as sedation and headache.7–9 
Additional effective non-drug therapies for PONV and 
postoperative pain enable minimization of pharmacological 
antiemetics and analgesics, and associated side effects.
Traditional Chinese medicine holds that acupuncture sup-
ports homoeostasis and minimizes the disturbances to normal 
function caused by surgery.10–12 Studies in human populations 
have shown that perioperative acupuncture minimized the 
elevation of serum serotonin,13 disrupted sympathoadrenal 
responses14 resulting from surgery, and reduced postoperative 
pain intensity and opioid analgesic consumption.15–17
Acupuncture has been included in international man-
agement guidelines for PONV management as the only 
non-pharmacological intervention.9,18 A 2015 Cochrane 
systematic review of 59 trials found that stimulation at the 
traditional acupuncture point location PC6 (Neiguan) was 
significantly better than sham acupuncture and reduced 
both PONV incidence and the requirement for “rescue” 
antiemetic treatment by one third.19,20 Despite low-quality 
evidence, the review stated that further sham acupuncture 
trials would not change the conclusion that acupuncture 
effectively reduces PONV. It recommended that further tri-
als should compare the effects of acupuncture plus multi-
ple antiemetic interventions with the latter alone.
Our pilot study in 40 patients at moderate to high risk of 
PONV found that, under best anesthesia practice, one in five 
patients at high risk still experienced PONV and that addi-
tional acupuncture could provide significant relief for this 
cohort.21 Acupuncture provided 24 h before surgery resulted 
in 25% and 75% reductions in postoperative nausea (PON) 
and postoperative vomiting (POV), respectively.
Few studies have evaluated the optimal timing or dosage 
of acupuncture on post-surgical recovery, and results have 
been inconsistent.12,22,23 Acupuncture was delivered 30 min 
prior to surgery in many trials,24–26 but may be stressful to 
patients as it is close to the time of surgery. Acupuncture 
one day before surgery produced better pain reduction than 
30 min prior in a previous study27 and was well accepted by 
both clinicians and patients in our feasibility study. It is not 
known whether effectiveness would be increased when 
combined with a second session 30 min prior to surgery; 
however, two sessions were found to be necessary to allevi-
ate chronic insomnia.28
Herein, we conducted a single-center, four-arm rand-
omized, controlled study in patients undergoing elective 
laparoscopic gynecological surgery with multimodal 
antiemetic prophylaxis. We aimed to: (1) evaluate the addi-
tional effects of preoperative electroacupuncture (EA) on 
PON and POV, and postoperative pain, compared with 
multimodal antiemetics alone; and (2) compare the effects 
of EA administered 24 h or 30 min before surgery, or a com-
bination of both, on PONV and pain.
Methods
This study was reviewed and approved by the Human 
Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of 
Nanjing University of Traditional Chinese Medicine 
(2016NL-019-02), and registered with the Chinese Clinical 
Trial Registry at http://www.chictr.org.cn/listbycreater.
aspx (ref. ChiCTR-INR-16010035) on 29 November 2016. 
All participants in the study were voluntary, understood the 
procedures, and provided written consent. The study hospi-
tal is a 2500-bed teaching hospital in Nanjing. More 
detailed methods are available in the published protocol.29

preoperative EA on PONV, compared with modern multi-
modal antiemetics alone. According to our pilot study,21 we

Between December 2016 and February 2018, 420 patients

were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were: 
American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I or II; 
general anesthesia; female sex; age 18–60 years; non-
smoking; body mass index 20–30 kg/m2; undergoing lapa-
roscopic hysterectomy, myomectomy, oophorectomy, 
salpingoopherectomy or ovarian cystectomy. Exclusion 
criteria were pregnancy or breastfeeding; psychiatric disor-
ders; substance abuse; chronic pain syndromes; use of 
antiemetic, opioid or glucocorticoid medications within 
3 days of surgery; nausea or vomiting within 24 h of sur-
gery; cardiac pacemaker; infection near the planned nee-
dling sites; surgery lasting over 3 h; conversion to an open 
procedure; or intraoperative bowel damage. The number of 
PONV risk factors was calculated using the simplified 
Apfel score.30 The four risk factors are: female gender; 
non-smoking status; history of PONV or motion sickness; 
and postoperative opioids (with a score of 0 representing no 
risk and 4 the highest risk). For every additional risk factor, 
the likelihood of PONV increases by 20%.
Eligible participants were randomly assigned to one of 
the following four groups using a computer-generated ran-
domization sequence and secure code: (1) Group Pre, who 
received EA the day before surgery; (2) Group 30, who 
received EA 30 min before surgery; (3) Group Comb, who 
received EA both the day before and 30 min before surgery; 
and (4) Group Usual, who received usual care. EA was 
given on the ward the day prior to surgery, and in the Pre-
anesthesia Care Unit 30 min before surgery, since surgical 
patients in China are routinely admitted to hospital 1–2 days 
prior to surgery. Each number was printed and stored in 
sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Eligible 
participants chose one envelope, which was opened by the 
acupuncturist who recorded the treatment allocation and 
provided the relevant procedure. Participants were 
instructed not to disclose their treatment.
Neither the anesthetists/surgeons nor the assessors were 
aware of the group allocation. Patients undergoing surgery on 
the same day were allocated to different rooms on the ward 
before surgery. All clinical outcome measures were conducted 
by a research nurse who was blinded to group allocation.
Acupuncture was administered at bilateral PC6, 2 cun or 
three finger breadths proximal to the wrist crease on the 
medial forearm, and ST36 (Zusanli), 3 cun or four finger 
breaths distal to the base of patella and one finger breadth 
lateral to the anterior border of the tibia.31 Point locations 
were selected based on Chinese medicine theory, previous 
trials and our feasibility study.21,32,33 After skin disinfection, 
stainless steel acupuncture needles (Suzhou Medical 
Supplies Factory Co. Ltd, 0.30x40 mm, China) were 
inserted by a doctor qualified in acupuncture and both 
Chinese and Western medicine, and manipulated until the 
patient reported de qi sensation (a radiating feeling of 
swelling or heaviness). The electrodes were connected 
from PC6 to ST36. An electric current was delivered for 
30 min in dense-disperse mode (20–100 Hz) using a 
stimulator 
(XS-998B, 
Nanjing 
Xiaosong 
Medical 
Instrument Research Institute, Nanjing, China) at a level 
where participants reported a strong but not painful sensa-
tion. Participants in the usual care group received no EA. 
All EA groups also received usual care.
Before induction of anesthesia and during surgery, elec-
trocardiography, heart rate, pulse oximetry, non-invasive 
arterial pressure, bispectral index (BIS) and end tidal carbon 
dioxide (CO2) were monitored continuously. General anes-
thesia was induced with intravenous midazolam 0.05 mg/kg, 
propofol 2–3 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.2–0.4 µg/kg, vecuronium 
0.1 mg/kg, and lidocaine 1 mg/kg. Total intravenous anesthe-
sia with continuous propofol and remifentanil was used for 
anesthesia maintenance. Intermittent sufentanil and vecuro-
nium were given as required. BIS ranged from 40 to 65. 
Volume-controlled ventilation was used and regulated to 
maintain end-tidal CO2 between 4.3 and 5.1 kPa. 
Dexamethasone 5 mg and tropisetron 5 mg were adminis-
tered at induction and immediately after skin closure, respec-
tively, to prevent PONV in all groups. Intravenous parecoxib 
40 mg was used for adjunctive pain management at the end 
of surgery. Patients were extubated when adequate spontane-
ous ventilation was established. Flurbiprofen 100 mg was 
administered when the numerical rating scale (NRS) pain 
score was >3, and metoclopramide 10 mg was infused as an 
antiemetic “rescue” therapy to any patient who experienced 
nausea, vomiting or both, and requested medication.
Outcome assessments
Patients were followed up while remaining in hospital 6, 
24, 48 and 72 h after surgery. The primary outcome meas-
ures were the incidence of PON and POV at 6–24 h after 
surgery, and pain on movement at 24 h. All patients were 
asked “did you feel nauseous/did you vomit in the time 
period since the last consultation?” The rate of reduction in 
PON and POV was calculated as: (incidence in Group 
Usual − incidence in EA groups)/incidence in Group Usual. 
A NRS (0–10) was used to rate pain, with 0 indicating no 
pain, and 10 the worst possible pain.
The secondary outcomes included severity of PON and 
POV; number of patients requiring postoperative rescue 
antiemetics and analgesics at 0–6 h, 6–24 h, 24–48 h and 48–
72 h after surgery; and incidence of PON and POV at 0–6 h, 
24–48 h and 48–72 h. Severity of nausea and vomiting were 
separately assessed using a 4-point Likert-type severity 
scale, where 0 = no nausea or vomiting, and 3 = the worst 
imaginable nausea or vomiting, as in a previous study.34
Other secondary outcome measures were the time inter-
val between the end of surgery and first flatus, and the vali-
dated Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) questionnaire.35
Any adverse events of acupuncture such as pain, infec-
tion, bleeding and fainting during the procedure15 were 
recorded by the acupuncturist at the end of the treatment 
and on the following two days.

Missing data and statistical analysis
An expectation-maximization procedure was used to deal 
with missing data, as a method to “best guess the missing 
value based on the specific model and the existing data 
points.”36
The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 
for Windows version 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) 
was used to analyze the data. Normality of the data was 
tested by Kolmogorov–Smirnov analysis. Continuous vari-
ables with complete or approximately normal distribution 
are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Chi-
square test or Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine the 
differences between groups in categorical variables, and 
analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test 
was used to compare descriptive continuous data among 
groups. A Bonferroni correction was performed when mul-
tiple paired comparisons were conducted. All reported p 
values were two-sided and p < 0.05 was considered statisti-
cally significant, except for the three primary outcome 
measures, for which the p value for statistical significance 
was adjusted to 0.017 to account for multiple testing.
Results
A total of 413 patients were enrolled and randomly allo-
cated to the 4 groups, and 400 patients completed the study. 
Thirteen patients were excluded because they were lost to 
follow-up (n = 3), did not complete the acupuncture inter-
vention (n = 2), or converted to open surgery (n = 8). The 
flow chart of the study is shown in Figure 1.
There were no differences between groups in preopera-
tive status, Apfel scores, or surgery and anesthesia-related 
parameters (Table 1).
Primary outcomes: incidence of PON and 
POV, and pain at 24 h
The primary outcomes are shown in Table 2. Incidence of 
both nausea and vomiting peaked in all four groups between 
6 h and 24 h postoperatively.
Between 6 and 24 h, Group Usual reported a nausea rate 
of 28% and vomiting rate of 23%, while nausea and vomit-
ing rates in the EA groups Pre/30/Comb were 11/18/11% 
and 5/8/9%, respectively. Accordingly, EA reduced the 
incidence of nausea from 6 to 24 h by 61/34/60%, and the 
incidence of vomiting by 79/65/61%, for Groups Pre/30/
Comb, respectively. The group differences were statisti-
cally significant (nausea: p = 0.003, vomiting: p < 0.001).
Post hoc analyses showed that at 6–24 h the incidence of 
PON in Groups Pre and Comb were less than half that 
recorded in Group Usual (Pre vs Usual: p = 0.024; Comb vs 
Usual: p = 0.024, Bonferroni corrected). Incidence of POV 
in Group Pre and Group 30 was less than one third of that 
reported in Group Usual (Pre vs Usual: p = 0.002, 30 vs

Severity of pain (Table 3) was mild over 72 h with high-

Secondary outcome measures
Tables 2 and 3 list the results of secondary outcome meas-
ures. Incidence of nausea in Group Usual at 0–6 h was 21%, 
with 18% experiencing vomiting. EA groups Pre/30/Comb 
reported rates of nausea and vomiting of 9/11/7% and 
7/9/6%, respectively. Accordingly, EA reduced the inci-
dence of nausea and vomiting from 0 to 6 h by 57/46/66% 
and 61/48/66%, respectively, for Groups Pre/30/Comb 
when compared with Group Usual. The four groups were 
significantly different in incidence of nausea (p = 0.013) 
and vomiting (p = 0.021).
An intention-to-treat analysis was performed on PON and 
POV incidence at 0–6 h and 6–24 h including all 405 patients 
who were randomized and did not convert to open surgery 
(Supplemental Table 1). The results were similar to the per-
protocol analysis as reported above (Supplemental Table 2).
Significant differences in the number of patients requir-
ing rescue antiemetics were shown between the four groups 
at 0–6 h (p = 0.001) and 6–24 h (p < 0.001) with Group 
Usual having the highest rate.
Post hoc analyses showed that antiemetic requirements 
in Groups Pre and Comb were significantly lower than in 
Group Usual at 0–6 h (Pre vs Usual: p = 0.012, Comb vs 
Usual: p = 0.013), and 6–24 h (Pre vs Usual: p = 0.003, 
Comb vs Usual: p = 0.007).
There were statistically significant differences between 
the groups at 0–6 h (p = 0.024) with those receiving usual 
care recording the highest pain levels. Scores in Group Pre 
were significantly lower than those in Group Usual 
(p < 0.05; Table 3). No between-group differences were 
observed in pain at 48 or 72 h. There were no statistically 
significant differences between the three EA groups for any 
other secondary outcomes.
The time until passage of first flatus exhibited a signifi-
cant inter-group difference (p = 0.003) with Group Usual 
having the longest duration (Table 3).
No significant differences were seen between the four 
groups in severity of PON or POV, incidence of postopera-
tive analgesic use, or QoR-15 scores.
Adverse events
Three patients in Group Comb experienced pain and itch-
ing at the needling sites, and two declined the second EA 
treatment. These minor and tolerable symptoms lasted 
<2 h, did not cause persistent visible signs, and did not 
require medical attention. During the 3-day follow-up, no 
further adverse events were reported.
Discussion
In the present single-center, randomized, assessor-blinded 
clinical study among patients undergoing elective laparo-
scopic gynecological surgery, preoperative EA stimulation 
at ST36 and PC6 reduced the incidence of PON and POV at 
6–24 h by 34%–61% and 61%–79%, respectively, and 
decreased rescue antiemetic use within 24 h. Early postop-
erative pain was reduced in the EA groups, as was the time 
to passage of first flatus. Postoperative pain was mild over-
all, therefore the differences in pain reduction between 
groups, while statistically significant for early pain, were 
not clinically relevant. The discussion hereafter therefore 
focuses on PONV.
No significant differences were detected between the 
three EA groups employing different timings of EA. 
However, when compared with Group Usual, Group Pre had 
a consistently lower incidence or severity of PON and POV 
and a lesser requirement for antiemetics at 6–24 h when 
PONV peaked.
Interpretation of findings: the combined 
intervention of EA and antiemetics
In our study, the incidence of PON and POV was 28% and 
23%, respectively, at 6–24 h in the usual care group without 
EA, which is consistent with the rate of 25%–30% in high-
risk patients receiving two intraoperative antiemetic drugs 
as prophylaxis reported in previous studies.37 It is not ideal 
that more than one quarter of patients still experienced 
PONV despite a multimodal strategy.
Combined therapy with acupuncture and modern 
antiemetics may offer additional reduction for those patients. 
Indeed, the 2015 Cochrane review concluded that the effect 
of acupuncture was equivalent to antiemetics.20 However, 
evidence is lacking as to whether a combination of acupunc-
ture and antiemetics is better than standard antiemetics 
alone. Previous studies have provided conflicting evi-
dence,20,38 and the Cochrane review called for comparative

Interpretation of findings: timing of EA
To our knowledge, no study to date has investigated the 
effects of two different timing schedules of preoperative 
EA, or compared one versus two sessions. Although we 
were not powered to examine equivalence, we found no 
suggestion that two sessions of EA were superior to one 
session of EA at any time point, and results were not sig-
nificantly influenced by the timing of EA. Therefore, a sin-
gle preoperative EA treatment is recommended. Based on 
our data, 350 patients would have been required in each 
group to provide definitive answers regarding the effect of 
EA timing. Optimal timing is also likely to be influenced by 
local factors including cost, availability of clinicians, time 
constraints and cultural acceptance. These considerations 
should be explored in future studies.
Gastrointestinal function is an important parameter for 
postoperative recovery and may promote the prevention of 
PONV, while PONV reduction may accelerate the recovery 
of intestinal ileus. EA has been shown to reduce the dura-
tion of postoperative ileus compared with sham after lapa-
roscopic surgery for colorectal cancer.41 Therefore, EA may 
reduce PONV directly, or indirectly by regulating gastroin-
testinal function. Our study also supported the analgesic 
effects of preoperative EA, which was similar to our previ-
ous feasibility study and the conclusions of several system-
atic reviews.42
The side effects attributable to EA in our study were 
minimal and resolved spontaneously. This is an important 
consideration, since the combination of different analge-
sics may lead to an increase in adverse effects. Therefore, 
preoperative EA may be a safe adjunctive antiemetic 
method.
Limitations
This study was limited to one hospital and patients under-
going a short duration surgery, and our findings may not 
extend to other procedures or longer durations. However, 
the adoption of standard anesthesia and EA procedures to 
patients undergoing a common surgery enhances the repli-
cability of this study. In addition, since this study focused 
on patients who were at high risk of PONV, the results are 
clinically relevant.
We recorded the incidence of rescue analgesic drug use 
but not the dosage, which might potentially have shown 
differences between groups. The sample size was sufficient 
to observe the effectiveness of preoperative EA in PONV,

Conclusion
Preoperative EA significantly decreases the incidence of 
PONV in patients undergoing laparoscopic gynecologi-
cal surgery. One session of EA at ST36 and PC6 appears 
to be a safe adjunctive antiemetic method for PONV 
prophylaxis. The optimal timing of EA requires further 
investigation.
