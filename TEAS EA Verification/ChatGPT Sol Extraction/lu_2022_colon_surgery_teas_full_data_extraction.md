# 24. Major methodological / audit issues

1. **100 randomized but only 94 analyzed**; six randomized patients did not receive intervention and were excluded.
2. The analysis is described as **modified ITT**, not a full ITT of all randomized patients.
3. Participant blinding was **not realistically maintained**; the authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation.
4. The control group received **no ongoing electrical stimulation**, although threshold testing/electrode placement was used to reduce expectancy bias.
5. Acupoint reporting is ambiguous: electrodes were placed at **PC6/LI4 and ST36/SP6**, while active stimulation is described as occurring at **PC6 and ST36**.
6. The study demonstrates a strong reduction in **PCA attempts and deliveries**, but **does not report the PCA drug or dose** in the main article.
7. Therefore the apparent opioid-sparing effect **cannot be converted to an exact 24-h opioid dose/MME** without supplementary or author data.
8. Pain scores were **not significantly different**, supporting that lower PCA use was not accompanied by clearly worse pain.
9. PONV was not improved and was numerically higher in TEAS at each reported time point.
10. **QoR-15 reporting is internally inconsistent** between the prose and Table 3.
11. Table 3 indicates a statistically **lower 24-h QoR-15 score in TEAS**, which would suggest worse early recovery on that instrument despite better GI recovery.
12. The main endpoint is **ERAS/discharge recovery**, not opioid consumption.
13. The study is relatively small but has the strength of being **multicenter**.
14. Follow-up for the reported perioperative outcomes was short (principally first 72 h / discharge).
15. Postoperative cost P-value differs slightly between table and prose (**0.21 vs 0.22**).

# 26. Concise Covidence-ready summary

**Lu et al. 2022** was a three-center randomized trial in China evaluating perioperative **TEAS added to an ERAS pathway after laparoscopic colon surgery**. One hundred participants were randomized, but only **94 received the allocated intervention and were analyzed (47/group)**.

Electrodes were placed bilaterally at **PC6/LI4 and ST36/SP6**. The authors describe active TEAS at **PC6 and ST36**, using a **2/10-Hz disperse-dense waveform** with stimulation threshold in the **4-11 mA** range. TEAS was given for **30 min before anesthesia induction** and then **30 min each morning for three postoperative days**. There was no intraoperative TEAS.

TEAS significantly improved several ERAS outcomes:
- time to discharge: **6.0 ± 1.8 vs 7.0 ± 2.4 days**
- first flatus: **34.5 ± 16.7 vs 42.4 ± 22.9 h**
- first defecation: **42.0 ± 25.0 vs 55.4 ± 29.7 h**

For the opioid-sparing review, this study has a strong **PCA-use signal**. At 24 h, median PCA attempts were **8.0 vs 16.5** and successful deliveries **5.0 vs 12.0**, both **P=0.001**. Similar reductions persisted at 48 and 72 h.

However, the main article does **not identify the PCA drug, opioid concentration, bolus dose, basal infusion, or lockout interval**. Therefore the reduced PCA deliveries cannot be converted into a reliable **24-h opioid dose or MME** from the main publication alone.

Importantly, postoperative pain did **not differ significantly** between groups despite fewer PCA deliveries. PONV was also not significantly different. The main audit issues are incomplete participant blinding, exclusion of 6 randomized participants from the modified-ITT analysis, ambiguity in the exact stimulated acupoints, and a major **24-h QoR-15 discrepancy between the prose and Table 3**.
