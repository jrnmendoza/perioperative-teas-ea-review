An estimated 312.9 million operations were performed worldwide in 2012, a
33.6% increase over eight years, with the largest proportional growth in the
countries spending least per capita on health care [@weiser2015]. Postoperative
analgesia remains inadequate for many of these patients. In a US national survey
of adults who had undergone surgery, 86% reported postoperative pain, and
three-quarters of those described it as moderate or extreme in the immediate
postoperative period; 88% received analgesic medication, and 80% of those
reported adverse effects from it [@gan2014]. Contemporary registry data show the
problem persists at scale and beyond that setting: among 26,193 adults in a
Chinese national postoperative pain registry, 48.7% reported moderate-to-severe
pain on the first postoperative day and 68% received systemic opioids
[@liu2023china]. Poorly controlled postoperative pain and the drugs used to
treat it both contribute to delayed recovery, prolonged hospitalisation and
avoidable morbidity [@kehlet2003].

These observations motivate opioid-sparing analgesia, but the rationale deserves
to be stated precisely. In a US cohort of previously opioid-naive adults, new
persistent opioid use occurred in 5.9% after minor and 6.5% after major surgery,
against 0.4% in a non-operative comparison cohort [@brummett2017]. That study
also found the incidence similar across surgical severity and more strongly
associated with behavioural and pain disorders than with the magnitude of
surgery, leading its authors to conclude that persistent use is not simply a
consequence of surgical pain. A subsequent meta-analysis of 43 observational
studies (6,507,173 participants) reached a comparable pooled estimate of 7.15%
(95% CI 6.02–8.38) but with a 95% prediction interval of 1.34–17.02%, again
found no significant variation by surgery type, and showed the figure to be
strongly definition-dependent: 8.29% under a 90–180-day definition against 2.89%
where opioid use across a full 180-day period was required [@frangakis2026].
Estimates are also markedly lower outside the United States — 1.7% in a national
Austrian cohort of 559,096 patients [@bologheanu2025] — and a pooled analysis
restricted to opioid-naive participants at baseline gave 1.2% (95% CI 0.4–3.9)
[@lawal2020].

Two things follow. Reducing in-hospital opioid administration should not be
assumed to reduce persistent use, and North American incidence figures should
not be read as globally representative. The firmer case for opioid sparing is
the immediate one: opioid side-effects, tolerance and opioid-induced
hyperalgesia are dose-related, and multiple clinical guidelines now endorse
multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia on that basis [@adams2023].

Transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS) and electroacupuncture
(EA) have both been proposed as non-pharmacological adjuncts to that end. They
are physically distinct interventions that are often grouped together: TEAS
delivers current through surface electrodes placed over acupuncture points
without penetrating the skin, whereas EA delivers it through inserted needles.
Electrical stimulation at acupuncture points releases endogenous opioid peptides
in a frequency-dependent pattern, with low-frequency stimulation favouring
enkephalin and β-endorphin and high-frequency stimulation favouring dynorphin
[@han2003; @han2004], and is also associated with anti-inflammatory and
autonomic effects [@lin2022; @wang2021]. The mechanism is plausible, and the two
modalities differ in a way — skin penetration — that bears directly on what a
credible sham can be, and therefore on what a trial can show.

Existing syntheses have not resolved whether either modality produces opioid
sparing of a magnitude that matters. Reviews to date have generally pooled
heterogeneous modalities into a single estimate [@wu2016review; @liu2025review];
one recent meta-analysis of electrical acupoint stimulation for postoperative
nausea and vomiting pooled electroacupuncture and transcutaneous electrical
acupoint stimulation into a combined effect and reported the two only as
subgroups of it [@lu2023review], although their sham comparators are not
interchangeable and their mechanisms of delivery differ. Syntheses restricted to
TEAS exist but are confined to single surgical populations and do not isolate
absolute opioid consumption: one in gynaecological surgery pooled
sham-controlled and conventional-analgesia comparisons and found no significant
reduction in pain intensity at low-to-moderate certainty [@tahmasbi2025], and the
most recent, in cancer surgery, reported pain as a standardised mean difference
across mixed comparators rather than in absolute units [@chu2026]. Two further
problems recur. Sham-controlled and usual-care-controlled comparisons are frequently
pooled, although only the former isolates the effect of stimulation from the
effect of the encounter. And opioid quantities that are not the same estimand —
a different time window, a weight-normalised dose, a pump volume, or a
patient-controlled analgesia total that omits separately administered rescue —
are converted onto a common scale and combined, so that the resulting estimate
inherits whichever conversion assumption is weakest.

Judgements of clinical importance have been correspondingly unstable. No gold
standard minimal clinically important difference exists for postoperative opioid
consumption, and thresholds are commonly chosen after the fact or displaced by
statistical significance. A systematic review of 570 analgesic trials after hip
and knee arthroplasty found a median clinician-perceived threshold of 10 mg
intravenous morphine equivalents over 0–24 hours (interquartile range 6.8–14.5),
and 40% for relative reductions (interquartile range 30–50%), while noting that
46% of trials with statistically significant primary outcomes did not reach
their own predetermined threshold [@laigaard2021]. Thresholds for opioid
consumption are not interchangeable with those for pain intensity [@myles2017],
and an apparent opioid reduction accompanied by worse pain is not opioid
sparing.

We therefore undertook a systematic review of randomised controlled trials of
perioperative TEAS and EA in adults undergoing surgery under general
anaesthesia. The registered primary outcome is all delivered systemic opioid
from the end of surgery to 24 hours in intravenous morphine equivalents, with a
co-primary pain outcome at approximately 24 hours and a registered joint
criterion for clinical importance requiring both a minimum opioid reduction —
prespecified at 10 mg, coinciding with the median clinician-perceived threshold
above — and pain evidence excluding clinically important worsening. TEAS and EA
were analysed separately throughout, sham-controlled comparisons were kept
separate from usual-care comparisons, and every candidate opioid result was
classified against the registered construct before any synthesis. The review was
designed primarily to examine whether protocol characteristics — timing,
repetition, cumulative stimulation, frequency and intensity — explain variation
in effect.
