## Protocol, registration and reporting

This review was registered with PROSPERO on 20 August 2026 (CRD420261452908),
following first submission on 19 August 2026; the registered record is version
1.0. Registration was verified against the public record on 20 September 2026
for title, authorship, dates and version history. The review start date recorded
in the registration is 14 August 2026, and we therefore do not describe this
review as prospectively registered. A separate, broader review by the same
author team is registered as CRD420251090635; that record does not describe the
present review.

Reporting follows PRISMA 2020 [@page2021statement; @page2021ee]. The completed
checklist is Additional file 2.

Four dated amendments were made after registration.

| Date | Amendment | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 14 Aug 2026 | Full-text outcome eligibility narrowed to reports providing 24-hour systemic opioid consumption and/or pain at approximately 24 hours | Narrower than the eligibility registered on 20 August, which admits any eligible outcome |
| 20 Aug 2026 | Eligibility restricted to English-language full texts; searches remain unrestricted by language | Separates search language from eligibility language |
| 11 Sep 2026 | Included *reports* distinguished from included *studies* | Counting only; no eligibility, outcome, method or estimate changed |
| 12 Sep 2026 | Standardised mean difference adopted as the headline measure for time to first flatus | Subsequently reversed (below) |

The 14 August amendment preceded, and is narrower than, the eligibility
registered on 20 August; we treat the difference as a potential protocol
deviation affecting the completeness of the evidence base. The 12 September
amendment was reversed: time to first flatus is reported as a mean difference in
hours within each modality–comparator stratum, with the standardised mean
difference retained only as a diagnostic. We judged hours to be the clinically
interpretable measure and did not choose between the two on the basis of effect
size, significance or heterogeneity; both are reported.

Every adjudication decision described below — estimand classification,
comparator classification, effect-measure choices, source holds, and risk-of-bias
and certainty judgements — was made after the results of the contributing trials
were known, and none should be read as prospective. The decisions, with dates and
rationales, are in Additional file 10.

## Eligibility criteria

Eligibility followed the registered PICOS, set out in full in Additional file
10. In summary:

**Population.** Adults aged 18 years or older undergoing operative surgery under
general anaesthesia, alone or combined with regional or neuraxial techniques, in
any specialty, elective or urgent. General anaesthesia was verified from the
source report during selection rather than imposed as a search concept, because
exploratory testing showed that a general-anaesthesia search block missed known
eligible reports whose anaesthetic technique was incompletely indexed. Where the
source did not establish it, the report was held rather than assumed eligible.

**Interventions.** Two modalities were eligible, defined by physical method
rather than by the label a trial applied to itself: **TEAS**, non-invasive
electrical stimulation through surface electrodes placed over recognised or
explicitly defined body acupuncture points without needle penetration; and
**EA**, electrical stimulation through one or more pairs of acupuncture needles
inserted at such points. At least one eligible session had to occur before the end of the first 24
postoperative hours; interventions could be delivered in any perioperative phase,
alone or adjunctive to balanced usual care. Manual acupuncture without electrical
stimulation, auricular and press-tack techniques, wrist-ankle and buccal
acupuncture, non-acupoint TENS, and mixed interventions whose independent TEAS or
EA effect could not be separated were excluded (Additional file 10). Because the
definitions are physical, a trial's own terminology did not determine its
classification, and reports that did not distinguish needle from surface
stimulation were held as modality-unclear.

**Comparators.** Comparator class was treated as part of the estimand, not as a
study characteristic, and classes were never pooled with one another. Principal
comparisons were TEAS versus inactive or credible sham TEAS and EA versus sham
EA; usual perioperative care, no stimulation, waiting-list and attention controls
were supportive; active electrical comparators were reported separately. Because
the registered record lists non-acupoint stimulation both as a form of credible
sham and as a possible active comparator, comparator class was assigned per
result from quoted source text rather than from a study-level label. Four trials
were reclassified on this basis (Additional file 10), and a broad-sham analysis
is retained as a diagnostic.

**Designs, setting and language.** Individually randomised parallel-group trials
were eligible, with cluster and crossover designs admissible under stated
conditions (Additional file 10); quasi-randomised, non-randomised and
observational designs were excluded, as were conference abstracts without
sufficient information to assess eligibility, risk of bias and outcomes. Any
country, health system and perioperative setting was eligible. Searches applied
no language restriction, but eligibility required an English full text; reports
published only in other languages were excluded during selection. We return to
this restriction in the Limitations.

## Information sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase (Embase.com, Elsevier), CENTRAL (Cochrane Library
Search Manager) and CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCOhost) through institutional access, on
21 July 2026 (PubMed, CENTRAL), 22 July 2026 (Embase) and 23 July 2026 (CINAHL).
Strategies combined terminology for electrical stimulation at acupuncture points
with surgical and perioperative concepts and validated randomised-trial terms. No
date, language, age, anaesthesia or outcome filter was applied. Subject headings
were validated in each platform's own browser before execution, and
substitutions, interface errors and access constraints were recorded in
per-database logs. Full strategies, counts and deviations are Additional file 1.

Only published studies were sought. One report was identified through citation
searching. The registered record also specifies searching trial and study
registers; **this was not completed**, and we report it as an incomplete element
of the registered search plan.

## Study selection

Records were managed in Covidence, deduplicated by documented automated and
manual procedures, with exports retained unchanged. Screening proceeded by title
and abstract, then full text, with exclusion reasons recorded against a
prespecified hierarchy; excluded full texts with reasons are Additional file 4.
The registered process is independent assessment by at least two people, or by a
person–machine combination, with a procedure for resolving differences.

Two features require explicit statement. Six reports previously recorded as
excluded were reinstated by later source-verified adjudication, including the
single trial contributing the principal primary-outcome estimate; these were
reconciled locally against the retained export records rather than by editing the
screening platform. And a difference of 12 references between the raw exports and
the recorded import totals could not be mapped to individual records; we report
it as an unexplained import gap rather than as duplicate removal. Upstream
deduplication totals reconcile arithmetically but cannot be replayed record by
record, because the platform event logs are unavailable.

## Data collection and source verification

Extraction captured study identifiers, setting, procedure, anaesthetic
technique, sample sizes, intervention and comparator descriptions, outcome
definitions and windows, arm-level statistics, and risk-of-bias information, with
protocol characteristics extracted against STRICTA-compatible domains
[@macpherson2010] (Additional file 10). Trials with incompletely reported
protocol characteristics remained eligible but contributed only to analyses for
which the relevant information was available. The registered process is
extraction by at least two people, or by a person–machine combination, with a
procedure for resolving differences.

Every source report was retained as a PDF pinned by SHA-256 hash, with a
plain-text extraction alongside. Each arm-level statistic entering any model was
checked literally against the source text or a registered supplement, with the
source location recorded per result; where a printed value could not be located
it was not used. Values were never altered or reconciled to make a source
internally consistent.

Author queries were drafted for eight source contradictions. **No author query
was sent**, and no analysis assumes or anticipates a response.

## Outcomes and the criterion for clinical importance

**Primary outcomes.** (1) Cumulative systemic postoperative opioid consumption
from the end of surgery to 24 hours, in milligrams of intravenous morphine
equivalents (IV MME), with mean difference as the principal effect measure;
programmed but undelivered patient-controlled analgesia doses are not counted as
consumed. (2) Postoperative pain at approximately 24 hours, assessed separately
at rest and during movement, on a 0–10 scale where a valid conversion existed.

**Additional outcomes.** Opioid consumption at later acute time points,
intraoperative opioid consumption, pain at other time points, nausea and
vomiting, rescue analgesia, quality of recovery, gastrointestinal and functional
recovery, length of stay, persistent opioid use, and adverse events plausibly
related to TEAS or EA (Additional file 10). Two distinctions were preserved:
nausea, vomiting and composite PONV were analysed separately rather than only as
a composite, and gastrointestinal recovery was separated into time to first
flatus, bowel sounds and defecation. The 48- and 72-hour opioid outcomes are
additional, not co-primary.

**Criterion for clinical importance.** The registered criterion is a conjunction:
an opioid-sparing effect is clinically meaningful only when *both* the point
estimate indicates a reduction of at least **10 mg IV MME** over 0–24 hours
**and** the corresponding pain evidence does not permit clinically important
worsening, prespecified as an increase of 1.0 point or more on 0–10 and assessed
against the upper 95% confidence limit. Rest and movement pain are evaluated
separately. Thresholds of 8 mg IV MME and, where calculable, a 30% relative
reduction are sensitivity interpretations that do not substitute for it.

Because the criterion is a conjunction it cannot be satisfied by an opioid
estimate alone. Where the trial contributing the opioid limb does not also
provide eligible ~24-hour pain evidence, the criterion cannot be evaluated for
that body, and pain evidence from other trials cannot supply the missing pairing;
we report this where it arises rather than substituting an unpaired comparison.

## Harms and patient-reported recovery

Adverse events plausibly related to TEAS or EA, and patient-reported recovery,
were addressed by a separate search-assisted coverage assessment of the available
full texts of all included reports, searching instrument names, satisfaction and
acceptability terms, local-reaction and general adverse-event statements, and
long-term opioid terminology. Matched statements were distinguished from
background claims, eligibility criteria and descriptions of other studies, and
every positive record carries a source-page anchor and a text hash. This was an
AI-conducted coverage assessment, **not** an independent second extraction; "not
located" is bounded by the available report text and does not establish absence.

Three reporting rules follow. Explicit statements of no events were recorded as
such and distinguished from absent reporting; a report without a safety statement
was **not** assigned zero events. Event types were not summed into counts of
unique affected patients without information on overlap, and events not plausibly
attributable to the needle or device — nausea, dizziness, respiratory depression
and surgical complications — were not counted as intervention harms. Because
definitions, windows, ascertainment and denominators differ across reports, **no
pooled comparative safety estimate was computed**. Harms and patient-reported
recovery are reported descriptively by instrument and time point, with fixed
postoperative times kept separate from discharge assessments.

## Conversion to intravenous morphine equivalents

The target unit is milligrams of intravenous morphine, not oral equivalents;
factors multiply both mean and standard deviation, and route and original unit
were checked per record. Factors applied were intravenous morphine 1,
hydromorphone 5 mg per mg and fentanyl 0.1 mg per µg. For intravenous sufentanil
we adopted **0.5 mg IV morphine per µg** centrally, with **0.25 and 1.0 as
mandatory sensitivity bounds** — the geometric midpoint of a published broad
parenteral equivalence range, chosen symmetrically on the ratio scale before the
final fits. It is not a validated constant for acute postoperative
patient-controlled analgesia, and the uncertainty is retained rather than
resolved. No principal contributor requires a sufentanil conversion.

Five conversions were prohibited outright, each requiring an unavailable
quantity: a participant's weight, an infusion duration, a drug concentration, a
record of what was delivered rather than programmed, or the individual-level
covariance needed to combine separately reported rescue components into one
standard deviation (Additional file 10).

Where no sourced factor existed, the trial contributed no IV MME value rather
than being converted on an assumed ratio. Eight further opioids, including
tramadol, pethidine and remifentanil, were not assigned universal postoperative
factors and were analysed in native units as distinct estimands; intraoperative
remifentanil is reported in micrograms. One trial's author-reported morphine-equivalent figure, whose oral or
intravenous basis could not be established, was retained only in a labelled
analysis assuming the author metric equals IV MME. Canonical rows retain the raw
unit, applied factor, route uncertainty and decision, so an unconverted row is
never silently treated as IV MME. The full register is Additional file 5.

## Classification of candidate results against the registered estimand

Trials frequently report an opioid quantity that resembles the registered primary
outcome without being the same estimand — a different time window, a
weight-normalised dose, a pump volume, a count of analgesia demands, a
patient-controlled analgesia total omitting separately administered rescue, or an
author-defined equivalent of unstated basis. Pooling such quantities requires
assumptions of varying defensibility, and the estimate inherits the weakest.

We therefore classified every candidate opioid result against the registered
construct — *all delivered systemic opioid from the end of surgery to 24 hours*
— before any synthesis, recording each classification with its source
justification. A result entered a principal or supportive body only where the
source established that it captured **all delivered systemic opioid exposure,
including any quantified rescue**; results whose systemic capture was incomplete
or uncertain were assigned to labelled sensitivity analyses instead. Permitted
but unquantified rescue was never treated as zero.

This classification is the principal reason the primary evidence base is smaller
than the number of included reports. Every candidate result's classification is
reported in Additional file 8, so readers can see which trials were excluded from
the primary bodies and why.

## Unit of analysis

Reports, studies and trial families were counted as distinct units; two reports
judged on source evidence to describe overlapping participants from one trial
were counted once as a single family and held out of every model.

Within any single evidence body a trial contributed exactly one contrast.
Compatible active arms were combined using the pooled mean and the
within-plus-between-arm standard deviation, or by summing events and
denominators, with the shared comparator counted once. Because modality and
comparator define separate estimands, a shared active arm could appear in more
than one body — in both a sham-controlled and a usual-care-controlled comparison,
say — but never twice in a single pooled body. Randomised, intention-to-treat,
modified intention-to-treat, per-protocol and outcome-specific denominators were
kept distinct.

## Statistical synthesis

Analyses were stratified by modality and comparator class. **TEAS and EA were
never combined in a pooled estimate**, and sham-controlled, usual-care and
active-comparator evidence were never pooled with one another.

Random-effects models for mean differences, log risk ratios and Hedges' *g* used
restricted maximum likelihood estimation of between-study variance. For k ≥ 2 we
applied Hartung–Knapp residual scaling safeguarded at 1, with a *t*(k−1) interval
and test; k = 2 bodies are interpreted as very uncertain. For **k = 1 we report
the study contrast with a within-study normal confidence interval, and make no
pooled random-effects claim, no heterogeneity estimate and no prediction
interval**. Prediction intervals were computed only for k ≥ 5, as
*t*(k−1) × √(τ² + SE²). Heterogeneity is reported as τ², I² and Cochran's *Q*,
with I² from the REML τ² and the typical sampling variance.

Dichotomous outcomes were pooled as risk ratios, with a continuity correction of
0.5 added to **all four cells** — so each arm denominator increases by 1 — and
only where a cell was zero; no universal correction was applied. Double-zero and
double-all-event studies were excluded from pooling and retained descriptively.

Direction conventions are fixed throughout: mean differences are intervention
minus comparator; a risk ratio below 1 denotes fewer adverse events; and a
negative mean difference for a gastrointestinal time-to-event outcome denotes an
earlier event, not a demonstrated improvement in global recovery.

Mean difference is the effect measure for opioid and gastrointestinal outcomes;
the standardised mean difference was a within-stratum diagnostic only and was
never back-transformed to a milligram scale. Missing standard deviations were
derived from standard errors, confidence intervals or test statistics only where
the derivation was exact. Median and quantile data were **not** converted for the
primary synthesis, because the available transformations assume an approximate
symmetry that was not tenable here; such data are reported in original form and
examined only in labelled sensitivity analyses.

## Protocol moderators

The registered review is designed primarily to investigate protocol-level effect
modification. The prespecified moderators are intervention timing; single versus
repeated treatment before the 24-hour outcome; cumulative active stimulation
duration before 24 hours, per 30-minute increment; electrical frequency; and
stimulation intensity. Their classification schemes are in Additional file 10.
Meta-regression was to be conducted separately for TEAS and EA, without causal
claims of superiority, and the registered record requires normally at least 10
independent estimates per predictor and four studies per categorical level, with
multivariable models considered only with at least 20 studies and 10 per
predictor.

**These thresholds were not met for any moderator within any
modality–comparator stratum, and no meta-regression is reported.** We did not
relax the thresholds, and did not carry forward an earlier mixed-modality
regression, because pooling TEAS with EA is prohibited by the registered design.
Following the registered fallback, protocol characteristics are synthesised
descriptively. The inability to execute the registered central analysis is
itself a finding and is reported as such.

## Sensitivity analyses and reporting bias

Prespecified sensitivity analyses restricting or excluding studies by sham
credibility, risk of bias, converted data, conversion uncertainty, unbalanced
co-interventions and influential studies were implemented as explicitly labelled
analyses, reported alongside and never in place of the primary bodies: the
sufentanil factor triplet; an expanded estimand admitting results of incomplete
systemic capture; leave-one-out omission of contradictory or high-risk trials;
standardised mean difference forms of the gastrointestinal outcomes; a broad-sham
analysis; and partial-window and weight-normalised forms in native metrics. A
restriction to studies at low risk of bias was **not possible**, because no
included trial was judged at low risk across all domains; the feasible analogue,
exclusion of high-risk results, is reported where it applies.

Risk of bias due to missing results was considered by comparing registrations,
protocols and publications. Funnel plots and small-study tests were considered
only where at least 10 sufficiently similar studies contributed; none is
informative for the principal comparison. Separately, we report a **review-level
selection concern**: the 14 August 2026 amendment excluded full texts not
providing one of the two primary outcomes, whereas the eligibility registered on
20 August admits any eligible outcome, and no dual independent re-screen was
undertaken. This limits completeness of the evidence base and is distinct from
publication bias.

**Post-hoc broadened opioid estimand.** After the primary results were known, we
defined a broadened construct (E2) that relaxes only completeness of opioid
capture. It admits patient-controlled analgesia totals and totals where rescue
opioid was given but not quantified, and it keeps the unit, the conversion
factors, the prohibited conversions and the separation of modalities and
comparators unchanged. E2 was fixed in writing before it was applied, and then
applied uniformly: to every extracted opioid result, and to every included report
without one. After it had been applied to those reports, and knowing which two
reports each change would admit, it was amended (E2.1). The amendment accepted
totals reported for "postoperative day 1" as the first 24 hours, and allowed
arm-level values printed only in figures to be digitised by pixel measurement
against the figure's axis gridlines. Each digitisation was checked against
values printed in the text, and disagreements were handled as source
contradictions. E2 was briefly adopted as the primary outcome and run; the
registered outcome was then retained as primary, a decision taken with the E2
results known. E2 is reported only as a sensitivity analysis. The definition,
amendment and decision are in Additional file 10, and the classification of
every report under E2 is in Additional file 12.

## Risk of bias in included results

Risk of bias was assessed with Cochrane RoB 2 [@sterne2019] across domains D1 to
D5, **at the level of the individual result** rather than the study: each exact
combination of outcome, time window, arm and analysis population was assessed
separately, because domain judgements differ across outcomes within the same
trial. The registered process is assessment by at least two people, or by a
person–machine combination, with a procedure for resolving differences.

Three rules were applied consistently and are stated because they are not
self-evident. Participants' awareness of allocation does not by itself make D2
high risk; the question is whether the assignment effect was altered. The
outcome's mode of ascertainment governs D4, so patient-reported nausea and pain
are assessed differently from observable vomiting and recorded analgesia dose —
an observable outcome is not automatically at high measurement risk because
participants knew their allocation. And pre-randomisation eligibility exclusions
are not attrition.

Where an assessment was unavailable the result was recorded as unassessed; no
judgement was imputed, and no result was assigned high risk merely because an
assessment was missing. Result-level judgements, signalling responses and source
locators are Additional file 6.

## Certainty of evidence

Certainty was assessed with GRADE [@guyatt2008] for each critical and important
outcome. Randomised evidence started at high certainty and was downgraded for
risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision or publication bias.
**No body was upgraded**; upgrading is not applicable to randomised evidence
under the registered plan, and none was applied for magnitude of effect or
dose–response.

Four rules governed the domain judgements. Downgrades rested on explicit domain
reasoning, not on a threshold applied to I² or a *P* value. A body was not
downgraded for inconsistency merely because it contained a single trial; where
consistency could not be evaluated, that was stated rather than penalised.
Imprecision for pain was judged against the registered ±1-point margin rather
than against statistical significance. And an opioid interval crossing both the
registered 10 mg and the supportive 8 mg threshold counted as one reason to
downgrade, not two. Sensitivity analyses and diagnostics were not separately
graded. Judgements are explained by domain in the Summary of Findings table and,
for all bodies, in Additional file 7.

## Handling of contradictory and uncertain source data

Source values were never altered. Where a report was internally contradictory,
one of three dispositions was applied and reported: **hold**, excluded from all
models with printed values retained in a labelled as-printed diagnostic;
**diagnostic only**, admitted to sensitivity analyses but not to a primary or
additional body; or **leave-out**, retained with an accompanying analysis
omitting it. Eight contradictions were handled this way and are itemised with
their dispositions in Additional file 10. Separately, one trial's intraoperative
opioid contrast was excluded from the causal analysis because randomisation and
treatment began on arrival in the recovery unit, after the intraoperative dose
had been given; it is retained only as a pre-intervention negative control.

## Software and reproducibility

Models were fitted in Python 3.14 using a restricted maximum likelihood
implementation built on NumPy and SciPy, and independently recomputed in R 4.5.2
with **metafor** 5.2-1 [@viechtbauer2010], re-deriving effect sizes and variances
from arm-level statistics rather than reusing the Python inputs, to a relative
tolerance of 1 × 10⁻⁶ for mean differences and risk ratios and 5 × 10⁻⁴ for
standardised mean differences. The chain is deterministic and regenerates
byte-identically from the canonical inputs. An independent validator, implemented
separately and re-deriving its inputs from source, checks canonical results,
model memberships, estimates, intervals and certainty assignments; it was
verified by negative control, rejecting a perturbation introduced during an
in-memory read while an unmodified run immediately afterwards passed. All source
reports and the registered protocol are pinned by SHA-256 hash.

We state plainly what this does not establish. A passing reproduction shows that
the reported estimates follow from the extracted data by the stated methods; it
does not verify that the source data are correct, that the extraction is free of
error, or that the evidence base is complete. Environment, commands, tolerances
and results are Additional file 11.

published with this review. We do not claim that every AI-generated output was

hashed form and every extracted value carries a source locator, so any individual
judgement can be checked against its source.
